Retirement account contribution limits get one round of real news each year — the IRS's official announcement, usually in November — but actuarial forecasts start circulating months earlier. Here's what's projected for 2027, what's already locked in, and how to plan around the uncertainty without overcommitting to a number that might shift.

## What's projected for 2027

Milliman's actuarial forecast projects the 2027 **401(k) elective deferral limit at roughly $25,000**, up from the confirmed **$24,500** in 2026. The "super catch-up" for savers age 60–63 is projected to rise to roughly **$11,750**, while the regular age-50 catch-up is expected to hold near **$8,000**. The overall defined-contribution limit, including employer contributions, is projected near **$75,000**.

## What's official vs. what's a forecast

It's worth being precise here, because these two categories get blurred in a lot of coverage. The 401(k) and IRA numbers above are **forecasts**, built from inflation data available through mid-year — the IRS sets the real numbers later, typically in a November notice. HSA limits work on a different, earlier timeline: the **2027 HSA limits are already official** at **$4,500 for self-only coverage** and **$9,000 for family coverage**, since the IRS confirms those well ahead of the calendar year.

<InfoBox type="warning">
Don't set a permanent payroll contribution percentage based on the 401(k) forecast alone. Actuarial projections are generally close, but they've been revised before once real inflation data came in. Plan around the forecast, then confirm and adjust once the IRS makes it official.
</InfoBox>

## Don't frontload past the match

Maxing out your 401(k) by, say, June feels efficient, but it carries a real risk: if your employer contributes its match **per paycheck** rather than "truing up" at year-end, front-loading contributions early can mean you stop contributing before the year ends — and miss months of employer match on the paychecks after you hit your personal limit.

## Here's What I'd Actually Do

1. **Confirm whether your plan trues up the match annually** before deciding whether to front-load contributions at all.
2. **Set your contribution rate as a percentage or flat per-paycheck dollar amount that spreads evenly across the year**, unless you've confirmed true-up protection.
3. **Prioritize the match first, then the Roth vs. traditional decision** — see my [Roth vs. traditional IRA comparison](/posts/roth-ira-vs-traditional-ira) for that trade-off.
4. **If you're 60–63, understand the mandatory Roth catch-up rule** before assuming pre-tax catch-up contributions are still available to you — see my [mandatory Roth catch-up breakdown](/posts/mandatory-roth-catch-up-2026).
5. **Revisit your contribution elections once the IRS confirms the real 2027 numbers**, typically in November — don't "set and forget" on a forecast.

If you're just getting started with investing rather than optimizing an existing plan, see [how to invest your first $1,000](/posts/how-to-invest-first-1000).
