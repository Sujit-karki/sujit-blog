Every benchmark on this site so far has measured arithmetic: a number is right
or wrong, and a script settles it. [Small local models failed the tax
calculations badly](/posts/ai-tax-brackets-more-context-worse-2026), and
[failed ordinary money math](/posts/local-ai-money-math-2026) more often than
their fluency suggests.

This one measures something people actually ask for. Not "what is 7% of
$42,500" but "where should this money go?" — a recommendation, with a correct
answer that has to be computed rather than recalled.

That question has a problem built into it. When a model says *529 plan*, there
are two entirely different things that could be happening, and from the outside
they look identical:

- **Computing** — working out the after-tax balance of each option and
  comparing them.
- **Reciting** — repeating the received wisdom that a 529 is the college
  account.

Both produce a confident, well-written paragraph. Both are frequently correct,
because the received wisdom is usually right. The only way to tell them apart
is to build a case where they disagree.

## Computing versus reciting

So I built one, and the trick is simple enough to describe in a sentence: ask
the same question twice, once with the account names attached and once with
them removed.

Both versions state every rule needed to reach the answer. The anonymous
version withholds nothing — same contribution, same growth rate, same tax
treatment, same time horizon, in the same order. It just does not say which
account is which.

A model that is computing must answer both the same way. There is no
information in the named version that the anonymous version lacks. Any gap
between the two is the size of the label's pull on the answer.

<InfoBox type="tip">
Everything here ran on my own machine through Ollama — an Acer Nitro 5 with a
GTX 1650 Ti, 4 GB of video memory and 7.8 GB of system RAM. Nothing was sent to
an API, and nothing was paid for. These are models small enough to run on
hardware plenty of people already own, which is the point: this is what the
free, private, offline option actually does.
</InfoBox>

## The only difference is the name

Here is one case in both framings — $2,000 a year for eighteen years, withdrawn
in a 22% bracket.

**Named:**

```
Option 1 -- taxable brokerage account
- You contribute $2,000 at the end of each year for 18 years.
- The balance grows at 7% per year.
- At withdrawal, every dollar above the total you contributed is taxed at
  the 15% long-term capital gains rate.

Option 2 -- 529 college savings plan
- You contribute $2,000 at the end of each year for 18 years.
- The balance grows at 7% per year.
- Withdrawals spent on qualified education expenses are not taxed at all.

Option 3 -- Trump Account
- A one-time $1,000 government deposit is made today and grows at 7% per
  year alongside everything else.
- You contribute $2,000 at the end of each year for 18 years.
- The balance grows at 7% per year.
- At withdrawal, every dollar above the total YOU contributed is taxed as
  ordinary income at 22%. The $1,000 deposit counts as growth, not as your
  own contribution.
```

**Anonymous:**

```
Option 1
- You contribute $2,000 at the end of each year for 18 years.
- The balance grows at 7% per year.
- At withdrawal, every dollar above the total you contributed is taxed at
  the 15% long-term capital gains rate.

Option 2
- You contribute $2,000 at the end of each year for 18 years.
- The balance grows at 7% per year.
- Withdrawals spent on qualified education expenses are not taxed at all.

Option 3
- A one-time $1,000 government deposit is made today and grows at 7% per
  year alongside everything else.
- ...
```

That is the entire difference. Three names deleted from three headings.

The correct answer to both is Option 2, and reaching it needs no judgement:
$2,000 a year growing at 7% for eighteen years is $67,998 in every account, and
the only question is what tax comes off the end. The 529 pays none. The
brokerage pays 15% on $31,998 of growth. The Trump Account starts $1,000 ahead
but pays 22% on everything above the $36,000 contributed.

## The eight cases

The cases straddle the crossover rather than sitting on one side of it. This
matters more than anything else in the design: if the 529 won every case, a
model that always says "529" would score 100%, and the benchmark would be
measuring received wisdom while claiming to measure arithmetic.

Four go to the Trump Account and four to the 529. The reason is worth knowing
independently of any AI: a free $1,000 seed compounding for eighteen years is
worth $3,380, and when your own contributions are small or your withdrawal
bracket is low, that seed outruns the tax the Trump Account pays on the way
out. Raise the contributions or the bracket and the 529's untaxed growth wins
instead.

Three of the eight are decided by under 1% of the final balance. Those are the
cases where a rule of thumb and an actual calculation come apart, and they are
in the test on purpose.

closed-form annuity formula and independently recomputed year by year in a
loop; the code refuses to run if the two disagree by more than a cent.
</InfoBox>

## What the names did

Deleting the names made the models better.

Named, the four models were right on 64 of 160 generations. Anonymous, on 75 of
160. Nothing was added to the anonymous prompts — the gap is what the labels
cost.

Three of the four models improved when the names came off. The fourth,
`qwen2.5:3b`, moved from 20 correct to 19, which is noise.

The sharpest effect is not in the accuracy at all. It is in which wrong answer
the models chose.

The taxable brokerage is a distractor, and a strict one. Under the stated
assumptions — the whole balance spent on qualified education — it is dominated
by the 529 outright: identical contributions, identical growth, and a
capital-gains bill the 529 simply does not pay. There is no contribution level
and no bracket in this test where picking it is defensible.

Models picked it **25 times out of 160 when it was named** and **4 times out of
159 when it was not**. Labelling the worst option "taxable brokerage account"
made it six times more attractive.

<InfoBox type="warning">
That is the finding I would take away from this. The name did not merely fail
to help — it actively pulled models toward an option that was strictly worse
than one sitting directly beside it, described in full, in the same prompt.
</InfoBox>

## The account with nothing in it

The `seed-only` case is the easiest question in the set. Contributions are
zero, so the 529 and the brokerage both finish with nothing at all. Only the
Trump Account has money in it, because only it receives the $1,000 government
deposit. There is no arithmetic to do — one option is funded and two are empty.

Named, the models got it right 5 times out of 20. Anonymous, 15 times out of
20.

Each model answered identically across all five repetitions, so this is not
randomness. `gemma2:2b` and `llama3.2:3b` both chose the 529 — the account
holding zero dollars. `phi3.5:3.8b` chose the empty brokerage. Only
`qwen2.5:3b` chose the funded account.

Here is how `llama3.2:3b` finished its working, verbatim. Option 1 is the 529,
Option 2 the Trump Account, Option 3 the brokerage:

```
Option 3: taxable brokerage account
- The balance grows at 7% per year for 18 years.
- The final balance is $2,549.19 (calculated using the formula for compound
  interest).
- The total amount contributed is $0, since no contributions were made.
- The amount above the contribution that is taxed is $2,549.19.
- The tax paid is $382.17 (15% of $2,549.19).
- The total amount withdrawn is $2,166.02 ($2,549.19 - $382.17).

Comparing the three options, we can see that Option 1 leaves the most money
to spend after tax, with a total amount withdrawn of $1,491.19.

ANSWER: 1
```

Earlier in the same reply it had put the Trump Account at $2,206.81. So it
finished holding three of its own figures — $1,491.19, $2,206.81, $2,166.02 —
and announced that the first one was the largest.

The comparison step is not a hard calculation that went wrong. It is three
numbers and a "greater than", and the model reached past its own arithmetic for
the familiar answer. (The $1,491.19 is itself invented: the 529 receives no
contributions and no seed in this case, so its balance is zero.)

## Difficulty did not predict anything

If the models were computing, accuracy would track how close the cases are. A
case decided by 30% should be easy; one decided by 0.23% should be hard.

The opposite happened.

The widest margin in the set after the empty case is `small-short`, where the
Trump Account wins by 29.87%. It scored **10%** — the worst of the eight, and
zero out of twenty under the named framing.

The tightest is `mid-long-low-bracket`, decided by 0.23% of the final balance —
a gap of about $155 on $67,998. It scored **62.5%**.

The best-scoring case, at 87.5%, is `standard`: $2,000 a year for eighteen
years, 22% bracket, answer 529. That is the canonical college-savings scenario,
the one any writeup of 529 plans uses as its worked example.

Accuracy did not follow the arithmetic. It followed how closely each case
resembled the textbook one — which is what reciting looks like when you measure
it.

## It was not choosing an account

Because the option order rotates between cases — the 529 is not Option 1
throughout, and neither is anything else — the slot a model picks carries
information its accuracy does not. Across the eight cases the correct answer is
Option 1 twice, Option 2 three times and Option 3 three times.

So a model that ignores the prompt and says "2" every single time scores
37.5%.

The four models scored 31.2%, 32.5%, 48.8% and 61.3% overall. Under the named
framing the whole set managed 40.0%. Two of the four models are below the
score you get for not reading the question, and the named framing as a whole is
barely above it.

The slot counts show why:

`llama3.2:3b` answered Option 2 on 85% of named prompts while the correct
answer moved around underneath it. That is not a model choosing an account. It
is a model with a favourite number.

Every model has one, each model's differs, and — the part that gives the game
away — the favourite *changes when the names come off*. `llama3.2:3b` goes from
85% on slot 2 to a near-even spread. `qwen2.5:3b` moves its preference from
slot 3 to slot 2. Whatever is producing these answers is sensitive to the
labels and largely insensitive to the numbers.

This is why the rotation is in the design. Without it, a model that always
answers "2" would have scored respectably on any test bank where the right
answer tended to sit in the middle, and nothing in its accuracy would have
revealed that it was not reading the options at all.

## What this can and cannot claim

The honest limitation is that anonymising removes the *name*, not the
recognisability.

"Withdrawals spent on qualified education expenses are not taxed at all" is the
529's defining feature. A model with real domain knowledge can identify the
account from that line whether or not the heading says so. So the anonymous
framing is not a clean-room test of pure arithmetic — it is a weaker
intervention than that, and the 6.9-point gap should be read as a lower bound
on the label's influence rather than a full measurement of it.

A second limit: five repetitions per prompt, not the ten used in the earlier
benchmarks. Valuing three accounts takes roughly four times the tokens of
answering one arithmetic question, and 320 generations at up to 2,048 tokens
each is already several hours on this hardware. Five is enough to distinguish a
consistent answer from a coin flip — and the answers here were highly
consistent, with most model-case cells identical across all five — but not
enough to put a tight interval around a rate.

Nor is this a test of the frontier models. It is a test of what runs free and
offline on a cheap laptop, which is a different and smaller question. A
$20-a-month model may well compute all eight of these correctly.

What the result does support: presenting the same arithmetic with familiar
names attached made four models measurably worse, made the strictly worst
option six times more attractive, and produced a pattern of scores that tracks
resemblance to the textbook case rather than the difficulty of the sum.

## Methodology

**Models.** `llama3.2:3b`, `qwen2.5:3b`, `gemma2:2b` and `phi3.5:3.8b`, all at
Q4_K_M quantisation, which is what `ollama pull` gives by default. One model
resident at a time, on a GTX 1650 Ti with 4 GB of VRAM.

**Design.** Eight parameter cases, each rendered in two framings — named and
anonymous — for sixteen prompts, run five times per model. 320 generations.

**The account rules.** Simplified from IRS
[Publication 970](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p970) and the IRS's
[Trump Accounts](https://www.irs.gov/trumpaccounts) guidance, then stated in
full inside each prompt. The simplification is deliberate and total: the test is
whether a model can compare three tax treatments it has just been handed, not
whether it has memorised this year's rules. Contribution limits, eligibility,
state treatment and fees are all excluded, which is why no row here should be
read as guidance about a real account.

**Determinism.** `temperature 0`, an explicit `num_ctx` of 2048 and an explicit
seed on every call. Seeds 42 through 46, one per repetition, because repeating
an identical call with an identical seed measures caching rather than
stability. One warm-up generation per model is discarded, because the first
call after a model loads reliably differs from later ones.

**Token budget.** `num_predict` is 2,048, not the 1,024 used in earlier
experiments. Valuing three accounts means three future-value calculations per
reply, and at 1,024 the smoke test truncated generations mid-working —
disproportionately on the *named* framing, where models spend tokens restating
account names. Truncation landing harder on one arm of a paired comparison
would have manufactured the finding outright. Every generation records Ollama's
`done_reason`; 7 of 320 hit the token ceiling.

**Grading.** Prompts require a final `ANSWER: <option number>` line. Where a
model ignored the format, the parser falls back to the last "Option N" mention
and then to the last bare digit, because a model that breaks the output format
is still expressing a choice and should be scored on the choice. Reported
separately from accuracy: 16 of 320 generations produced no `ANSWER` line, and
1 could not be parsed at all.

**Ground truth.** Computed from the closed-form annuity formula and
independently recomputed year by year in a loop, with the module refusing to
import if the two disagree by more than a cent. No model was consulted for a
correct answer at any point.

**Rotation.** The option order shifts by one position per case, identically in
both framings, so the named/anonymous comparison is unaffected by it while no
account sits in the same slot throughout.

**One contended tail, re-run.** The final three generations of the run were
produced while the machine was out of memory, and they collapsed: repetitive
gibberish, two of them reporting zero evaluated tokens despite eighty and
ninety-five seconds of wall time. One of the three was scored *correct*, because
the bare-digit fallback found a stray "3" inside the garbage. Re-run on an idle
machine, all three returned clean answers at 24 tokens per second, matching this
model's median. The contended versions are published unchanged as
`allocation-contended-tail.jsonl` rather than deleted, because a benchmark that
quietly drops its bad runs is not reproducible — and because a scoring
fallback that turns noise into a correct answer is worth showing.

**Reproducibility.** The experiment is
[`research/experiments/allocation.py`](https://github.com/Sujit-karki/sujit-blog/blob/main/research/experiments/allocation.py)
and the harness is
[`research/harness/experiment.py`](https://github.com/Sujit-karki/sujit-blog/blob/main/research/harness/experiment.py).
Raw generations, including every full reply, are published as
`allocation-raw.jsonl`; the paired per-case comparison is `allocation-pairs.csv`.
