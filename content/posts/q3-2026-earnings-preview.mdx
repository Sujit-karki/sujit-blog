## What actually happened

Nothing has "happened" yet in the reporting sense — Q3 earnings season traditionally starts in mid-October, when the big banks report first and the rest of the S&P 500 follows through November. What exists right now is the analyst consensus feeding into that season, tracked continuously by FactSet's widely-cited Earnings Insight report. As of mid-2026, that consensus projected roughly 27.4% year-over-year earnings growth for the S&P 500 in Q3, alongside about 11.3% revenue growth — both strong numbers by historical standards, and both estimates that will move, likely down somewhat, as the actual reporting season approaches and companies issue guidance.

Zooming out to the full year, FactSet's calendar-year 2026 estimate ran near 30% earnings growth with about 11.5% revenue growth. Whether that holds up matters beyond the headline — it's baked into the valuation multiple investors are currently paying for the index.

## The math for one person

Here's the part that actually matters if you hold an S&P 500 index fund rather than individual stocks: a forward P/E of about 20.0 against a 10-year average of about 19.0 means the market is paying roughly a 5% premium over its own historical norm for a dollar of projected future earnings. That's not an extreme premium by the standards of past bull-market peaks, but it's not cheap either, and it means the growth projections above aren't just interesting analyst chatter — they're already priced in.

If Q3 earnings come in at or above that 27.4% growth estimate, the current multiple looks reasonable in hindsight. If growth disappoints meaningfully below that number, the same multiple starts to look stretched, and index-level price weakness tends to follow disappointing aggregate earnings more reliably than it follows almost any other single input.

<InfoBox type="tip">
"Beat the estimate" headlines are less informative than they sound, because the "estimate" being beaten is often a lowered bar set by companies' own guidance in the weeks before reporting — a genuine beat against where analysts stood months earlier is the more meaningful signal, and it's harder to find in the headline coverage.
</InfoBox>

## Why estimates aren't guarantees

I'd be doing you a disservice presenting a 27.4% growth estimate as anything more than what it is: a snapshot of analyst opinion on a specific date, built from company guidance, macro assumptions, and models that get revised constantly. FactSet's own report updates weekly through earnings season specifically because the number keeps moving as new information arrives.

The concentration point deserves its own emphasis: a handful of the largest companies in the index — heavily weighted toward the same names driving AI-related capital spending — can move the aggregate growth number disproportionately relative to the other 490-plus companies in the S&P 500. A strong index-level number doesn't necessarily mean broad-based strength across every sector.

## Here's What I'd Actually Do

1. **Check the latest FactSet Earnings Insight report before relying on any specific number in this post** — these figures move weekly, and by the time you're reading this, actual results may already be rolling in.
2. **Don't treat a high forward P/E as a market-timing signal.** It's a valuation input, not a prediction of when or whether a pullback happens.
3. **Pay attention to breadth, not just the headline growth number** — check whether strength is broad-based or concentrated in a handful of mega-cap names before drawing conclusions about the broader economy.
4. **If you're a long-term index investor, resist reacting to any single quarter's beat or miss.** Systematic, scheduled contributions have historically outperformed attempts to time entries around earnings season.
5. **Read guidance commentary, not just the headline beat/miss**, if you follow individual earnings calls — forward guidance usually matters more to the stock's reaction than the backward-looking quarter.
6. **Revisit your own risk tolerance if the concentration story concerns you** — a broader, more diversified fund is a legitimate response to concentration risk without requiring any market-timing call at all.
