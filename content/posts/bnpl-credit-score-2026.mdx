If you've used Klarna, Affirm, or Afterpay and wondered whether it's quietly changing your credit score, the honest answer used to be "probably not, in either direction" — because most BNPL providers didn't consistently report to the major credit bureaus. That's starting to change. Let me walk through what's actually different now, what got reversed in a separate but related fight over medical debt, and what genuinely moves your score versus what doesn't.

## What BNPL scoring actually does

FICO announced **FICO Score 10 BNPL and FICO Score 10 T BNPL** on June 23, 2025, becoming available to lenders in the fall of 2025 — the first credit scores from a major provider built specifically to incorporate buy-now-pay-later loan data. FICO is used by roughly 90% of top US lenders, so a model change here has real reach once lenders actually adopt it.

The new models were trained on a joint study with Affirm covering more than **500,000 consumers**. The headline finding: for the large majority of people, adding BNPL data to the score doesn't swing it dramatically. On-time BNPL use moved scores within **±10 points for more than 85%** of the study population — comparable to the kind of shift you'd see from opening any new account.

<InfoBox type="info">
These new scores are offered *alongside* existing FICO models, at no extra cost to lenders, not as a mandatory replacement. That means adoption will be gradual and uneven — don't assume every lender checking your credit is already looking at BNPL activity.
</InfoBox>

## Medical debt: what changed and what got reversed

This is a genuinely separate fight that gets confused with the BNPL story because both involve "what counts on your credit report" debates happening at the same time. The credit bureaus made two voluntary changes years ago: **paid medical collections were removed and given a one-year reporting delay starting July 1, 2022**, and **medical collections under $500 were removed starting April 11, 2023**. VantageScore's newer models (3.0 and 4.0) fully exclude medical collections, and FICO Score 9 gives them reduced weight.

The bigger swing was a **CFPB rule, finalized in January 2025**, that would have gone further and removed most medical debt from credit reports and barred lenders from considering it at all. That rule never got the chance to take effect: on **July 11, 2025, the US District Court for the Eastern District of Texas vacated it** in *Cornerstone Credit Union League v. CFPB*, on a joint request from the CFPB itself and industry plaintiffs, finding the rule exceeded the agency's authority under the Fair Credit Reporting Act. The court's order also bars the CFPB from issuing a similar rule again. So the voluntary bureau changes from 2022-2023 still stand, but the broader federal rule is dead.

## What actually moves a score

I'd rather people worry less about BNPL and medical debt specifically and more about the two factors that dominate every mainstream scoring model regardless of what's newly included: **payment history** and **credit utilization**. Those two factors combined typically account for the majority of your score's movement in any given month. A single BNPL loan paid on time is a rounding error next to a maxed-out credit card or a 30-day-late mortgage payment.

<InfoBox type="tip">
If you're using BNPL as a substitute for a credit card because you think it's "off the radar," that assumption is getting weaker every quarter as more providers start reporting to bureaus and more scoring models are built to see it. Treat every BNPL installment like the real debt obligation it is.
</InfoBox>

For a broader look at how BNPL fits into household debt overall, I've also written about [the record $1.277 trillion in credit card debt and where BNPL fits into that picture](/posts/credit-card-bnpl-debt-2026) — useful context if you're carrying both.

## Which score is even being used?

Here's a wrinkle worth naming honestly: even where BNPL-aware scores exist, most lenders you interact with day to day — a mortgage lender, an auto lender, a new credit card issuer — may still be pulling an older score model that doesn't see your BNPL activity at all, because BNPL providers don't universally report to Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion the way traditional lenders do. That's likely to keep changing as the new models roll out, but it means the practical impact of your BNPL use on any specific application still depends heavily on which lender, which bureau, and which score version is actually being pulled.

## Here's What I'd Actually Do

1. **Pay every BNPL installment on time, every time**, and treat it exactly like a real credit obligation — because it increasingly is one for scoring purposes.
2. **Don't assume BNPL is invisible to lenders anymore.** That assumption is getting less true every quarter.
3. **Focus your actual score-improvement effort on payment history and utilization** — they still dwarf any single new-account effect, BNPL included.
4. **If you're managing old medical debt, know the bureaus' 2022-2023 changes still apply** even though the broader CFPB rule was struck down — paid and small medical collections are still generally excluded.
5. **Check which score a lender is actually pulling** before assuming a new BNPL-aware model applies to your specific application.
