Every "side hustle" article promises the same round number — a thousand dollars a month, like it's a light switch. I wanted to find out what the actual data says a college student can expect, because the gap between the promise and the median outcome is exactly the kind of thing that makes people give up after one bad month.

## The mean-vs-median problem

Before picking a hustle, it's worth sitting with one uncomfortable, well-documented fact about side income in general: the average number you see quoted is almost never the number most people actually experience.

Bankrate's 2025 side hustle survey (fieldwork June 2-4, 2025, roughly 2,600 US adults) found an average side hustle income of **$885/month** — but a **median of just $200/month**. That's not a small gap; it means a relatively small number of high earners are pulling the average way up, while a majority of people doing side work make far less than the headline figure suggests. Generationally, Gen Z side hustlers in that same survey averaged **$968/month**, closer to (but still not matching) the oft-quoted "$1,000/month" promise.

<InfoBox type="warning">
When you see a single "average side hustle income" statistic anywhere — in this post or elsewhere — ask whether it's a mean or a median. A large mean pulled up by a small number of big earners tells you almost nothing about what a typical beginner should expect in month one.
</InfoBox>

I don't have a rigorous, student-specific version of this same mean/median breakdown — most large surveys sample all working-age adults, not college students specifically — so treat the Bankrate numbers as the closest reliable proxy, not a student-only statistic.

Rather than another generic list, here are four common student hustles with the actual rate data I could find and verify, plus the honest caveats on each.

**Tutoring.** This has the most consistent rate data of the four. General 2026 market rates run about $25-80/hour depending on subject and level, with test prep and advanced STEM subjects at the top of that range. On Wyzant specifically, tutors average around $35-60/hour listed, with actual average payout closer to $34.60/hour once the platform's roughly 9% service fee is netted out. If you have a strong subject (math, a language, standardized test prep), this tends to be the highest, most stable hourly rate on this list for a beginner with no existing portfolio.

**Freelance work (writing, design, admin, light dev).** Upwork's platform-wide average rate is $47.71/hour — but that number blends everyone from total beginners to specialists with years of client history. A first-time freelancer without a portfolio should expect to start well below that average, not at it. Fee structures matter too: Upwork's fee is variable (0-15% depending on the client relationship), while Fiverr charges a flat 20%. I went deeper on realistic freelance earnings ladders — including how AI-related skills are shifting rates — in my [AI side hustles piece](/posts/ai-side-hustles-2026-real-numbers), which is worth reading before you set your first price.

**Food delivery (DoorDash, Uber Eats).** 2026 driver-tracking data puts Uber Eats around $24.68/hour gross on average and DoorDash around $18.93/hour gross, though GPS-tracked per-trip data for Uber Eats specifically shows a wider $14-22/hour gross range. The number that actually matters is net: after gas, vehicle wear, and the 15.3% self-employment tax, real take-home commonly drops to roughly $9-15/hour. This is the most capital-intensive hustle on the list (you need a working vehicle) and the one where gross and net diverge the most.

**Reselling (Depop, Etsy, thrifted goods).** This is the softest data point here, and I want to be upfront about that. Industry estimates (not a rigorous academic or government survey) put casual resellers around $50-200/month, and sellers who treat it as a consistent, run-like-a-business operation — regular listing, reinvesting in inventory, strategic pricing — in the $500-3,000+/month range. Etsy's spread is even wider, with a small top tier reportedly clearing $10,000+/month. Take all of these figures as directional, not precise.

<InfoBox type="info">
I looked for a rigorous student-specific reselling income survey and didn't find one I'd trust to cite here. If you find better primary data, treat my reselling numbers as a starting estimate to sanity-check against your own listings, not a benchmark to hit.
</InfoBox>

## The tax part nobody explains well

This is the part that trips up first-time side hustlers, and it's worth getting right before your first payment lands, not after.

**All of this income is taxable, whether or not a form shows up.** The IRS doesn't care whether PayPal, Venmo, or a client sends you a 1099 — income from services or goods sold with the intent to profit is reportable regardless. I went deep on exactly this distinction in my piece on [the $20,000 1099-K rule](/posts/the-20000-1099k-rule-is-back): the 1099-K threshold (more than $20,000 in gross payments **and** more than 200 transactions on a single platform, restored by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act) only governs when a *platform* must send you paperwork. It never governed whether you *owe* tax.

Two thresholds matter here, and they're easy to conflate:

**Self-employment tax is the number most students underestimate.** Once you're self-employed (not a W-2 employee), you owe 15.3% self-employment tax — covering Social Security and Medicare — on top of ordinary income tax on your net profit. Many students, especially those with little other income, end up in a low or even 0% federal income tax bracket, but the 15.3% self-employment tax still applies regardless of your income tax bracket. A 25-30% set-aside of net profit is a reasonable starting reserve to cover both.

<InfoBox type="tip">
Keep your own books from day one — a spreadsheet with gross payments, platform fees, supply costs, and mileage is enough at a small scale. It's the difference between confidently deducting real expenses on Schedule C and guessing at tax time.
</InfoBox>

## Here's What I'd Actually Do

1. **Pick the hustle that matches a skill you already have**, not the one with the flashiest headline rate. A subject you're already strong in (tutoring) or already do (writing, design) beats starting from zero in an unfamiliar skill.
2. **Track hours and gross pay from week one**, even in a basic spreadsheet. You can't sanity-check the mean-vs-median gap against your own results if you don't have your own numbers.
3. **Set aside 25-30% of every payment the moment it lands**, in a separate account. Don't wait until tax season to discover you spent the self-employment tax portion.
4. **Reconcile any 1099-K or 1099-NEC you receive against your own records** — gross figures on those forms are not your taxable profit; expenses come off first.
5. **Diversify past one platform if you rely on this income.** A single-platform outage, policy change, or account suspension shouldn't wipe out your entire month.
6. **Reassess after 90 days, not after week one.** Most side hustles — this matches the general freelance/AI-side-hustle attrition pattern I've written about before — take a few months to find their real rhythm; a slow first two weeks isn't a verdict on the whole hustle.
