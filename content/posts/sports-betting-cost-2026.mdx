## The numbers behind the boom

The American Gaming Association's State of the States 2026 report puts 2025 legal sports betting handle at **$166.94 billion**, up 11% from 2024. Sportsbooks kept **$16.96 billion** in revenue (up 22.8% year-over-year), and states collected **$3.71 billion** in sports-betting tax (up 32.4%). Total U.S. commercial gaming revenue hit a record **$78.72 billion** across every category.

That growth is staggering, but flip the frame: sportsbook revenue *is* bettors' aggregate net losses. The $16.96 billion sportsbooks earned is money that left players' pockets and didn't come back.

## The hold: why the house wins

Dividing sportsbook revenue by handle gives the "hold" — about **10% industry-wide** in 2025 ($16.96B ÷ $166.94B). That's the structural edge baked into the odds, often called the "vig" or "juice." A standard bet priced at -110 asks you to risk $110 to win $100 — the extra $10 is the house's cut, regardless of who wins that particular game.

Over one bet, variance dominates and you might win big. Over a season of dozens of bets, the hold grinds that variance down toward a near-mathematical certainty. This is the single most important idea in this post: **the edge isn't in any one game, it's in the repetition.** The more you bet, the more your results look like the industry average — and the industry average is a loss.

## The tax trap nobody mentions

Here's what surprises people at tax time. In the U.S., **all** gambling winnings are taxable income — reported on your federal return whether or not you receive a Form W-2G. You can deduct losses, but only if you **itemize**, and only up to your winnings. If you take the standard deduction — most filers do — you owe tax on winning bets and get **no offset** for losing ones.

<InfoBox type="warning">
If you win $2,000 across a season and lose $2,500, and you take the standard deduction, you can still owe income tax on the $2,000 in wins — while eating the full $2,500 in losses yourself. The tax code doesn't net your bad beats against your good ones unless you itemize.
</InfoBox>

There's also a federal excise tax of 0.25% on the handle, paid by operators rather than bettors directly — but it's baked into the pricing and promotions you see, same as any other cost of doing business.

## The human cost

This isn't only about margins. After states legalized mobile betting, problem-gambling helpline calls surged in several of them — Massachusetts reported calls up 121% between 2022 and 2023, with sports-betting-specific calls up more than tenfold off a small base, per the state's Department of Public Health. New York's Comptroller noted a meaningful rise in problem-gambling calls after mobile betting launched there too. Younger men are the fastest-growing group of callers, per the National Council on Problem Gambling. If betting stops being fun, the national helpline is **1-800-GAMBLER**.

## Here's What I'd Actually Do

1. **Budget betting as entertainment, not investment.** Decide on a season number you'd be genuinely fine losing entirely — because on average, across enough volume, you will lose something close to the hold.
2. **Assume the ~10% hold is your cost of playing.** Bet $1,000 across a season and expect roughly $100 gone to the house before variance even enters the picture.
3. **Track every wager if you have real winnings.** You may need to itemize to deduct losses — talk to a tax professional before assuming anything nets out automatically.
4. **Never chase losses or bet with borrowed money.** That's the behavior that turns a hobby into [credit card debt](/posts/credit-card-bnpl-debt-2026).
5. **Redirect the "sharp" instinct into something with positive expected value** — a simple [50/30/20 budget](/posts/budgeting-50-30-20-rule) or the automatic-savings math in my [no-buy year breakdown](/posts/no-buy-2026-the-math) both beat the house, reliably.
