## The financing options, decoded

There are four common ways to pay for a new phone, and they are not equivalent.

**Pay cash.** Simplest and cheapest. You own it outright, no strings attached.

**Apple's own installments.** Typically a genuine 0% monthly plan through a financing partner. If it's truly 0% and the payment fits your budget, this option is fine.

**Carrier installment plus "deal."** The phone's cost is split over 24–36 months of bill credits. The headline "free phone" is almost always contingent on staying on a specific plan for the full term.

**Buy Now, Pay Later.** Splitting a phone across four payments or a longer BNPL loan. Consumer regulators have repeatedly flagged that BNPL can encourage overextension, and some plans carry fees or interest that aren't obvious upfront.

## The trade-in credit math

Here's the mechanic that trips people up. A "$1,000 trade-in credit" is rarely $1,000 off at the register. It's usually **$1,000 spread across 36 monthly bill credits** — about $28/month. Switch carriers, pay off the phone early, or change plans, and you typically forfeit the unused credits. So the "deal" functions as a 36-month loyalty contract, priced to look like a discount.

<InfoBox type="tip">
Before accepting a trade-in offer, check the phone's real resale value on a marketplace. Sometimes selling it yourself beats the carrier credit — and it doesn't chain you to anyone.
</InfoBox>

## When 0% is actually free

A 0% plan is genuinely free money **only if two things are true**: you were going to buy the phone regardless, and the monthly payment fits your budget without crowding out savings or a credit card balance. If financing is what *lets* you buy a phone you couldn't otherwise afford, the plan isn't free — it's the thing enabling an overspend.

There's a fair counterargument here: if you'd earn more keeping your cash invested or in a high-yield account, financing at 0% can make sense even if you have the cash on hand. That's true, but modest — the interest on roughly $1,000 over a year is a few tens of dollars. The bigger risk is the multi-year carrier lock-in, not the financing rate itself.

## Here's What I'd Actually Do

1. **Ask if you actually need a new phone.** A battery replacement costs a fraction of $1,000+ and can buy years of extra life.
2. **If you do buy, prefer cash or a true 0% plan** you can pay off without touching your emergency fund.
3. **Read the trade-in terms before you accept them.** If the credit is spread over 36 months, treat it as a 36-month carrier commitment and price it that way.
4. **Avoid BNPL for a phone.** Stacking short-term installments on a big purchase is how a want turns into [credit card debt](/posts/credit-card-bnpl-debt-2026).
5. **Wait a beat after launch.** Prices and trade-in deals often shift in the weeks after a new model ships, and last year's model typically drops the same day.

For the psychology of skipping a purchase entirely, see my [no-buy year math](/posts/no-buy-2026-the-math); to fit a phone into a plan without guilt, the [50/30/20 budget](/posts/budgeting-50-30-20-rule) puts it squarely in "wants."
