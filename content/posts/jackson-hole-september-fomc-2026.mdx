I want to get past the headline you'll see out of Jackson Hole this month, because most of the coverage will tell you what Chair Warsh said and almost none of it will tell you what it means for the money sitting in your savings account. So let me do that instead, using what we actually know as of today — not a guess about what happens on a stage in Wyoming two weeks from now.

Here's the one-sentence version: the Fed just held rates for a second straight meeting, July's inflation print gave the doves a little more ammunition, and the next three weeks — Jackson Hole on Aug 27–29, then the September 15–16 FOMC meeting — will do more to decide your savings yield than anything that's happened so far this year.

## Where things stand after the July 29 hold

The Federal Open Market Committee met July 28–29 and, as prediction markets had priced at roughly 90% odds heading in, held the federal funds target range at **3.50%–3.75%** — the second consecutive hold after the June 17 meeting. The prime rate, which resets within a day of any FOMC move, stays at **6.75%**.

That's the calm part of the story. The more interesting part is what's happened to the inflation data since that July 29 statement went out — because it's shifted the conversation meaningfully in the three weeks since.

## July's cooler-than-expected CPI

The Bureau of Labor Statistics released the July Consumer Price Index this morning, and it continued the cooling trend that started in June. Headline CPI came in at **3.4% year-over-year**, down a tenth from June's 3.5%, while core CPI (stripping out food and energy) eased to **2.5%**, down from 2.6%. It's the second straight month of easing on both measures — not a dramatic move, but a real one.

Here's where it's worth being precise about what this print does and doesn't tell you. Two straight months of cooling is genuine progress, but core inflation at 2.5% is still half a point above the Fed's 2% target, and a new chair who spent his confirmation hearings promising discipline isn't going to declare victory on two data points. What it does do is give the doves on the committee a stronger hand heading into September than they had in July — which is exactly why the next three weeks matter more than usual.

## Jackson Hole: Warsh's first symposium as chair

The Kansas City Fed's Economic Policy Symposium runs **August 27–29, 2026**, at Jackson Lake Lodge in Wyoming — and this is the first time Chair Kevin Warsh, sworn in May 22, gives the keynote. It's small by design (roughly 100–140 attendees in recent years) but the guest list is central bankers, finance ministers, and academics from around the world, so what gets said there moves markets even though it's closed to the general public.

I need to be honest about what I don't know: I'm writing this two weeks before the speech, so I can tell you the setup, not the punchline. What I can tell you is why it matters more than a typical Jackson Hole. It's Warsh's first appearance at the symposium as Chair, which means every sentence gets read for how he frames the inflation-versus-growth trade-off differently than his predecessor did. And unlike a scripted FOMC statement negotiated by committee, a Jackson Hole speech is the Chair's own words, in his own venue, three weeks before a vote.

<InfoBox type="info">
Jackson Hole is a symposium, not a policy meeting. Nobody sets a rate there — the actual vote happens at the FOMC meeting on September 16. But because it's the Chair's biggest solo platform before that vote, markets treat it as the closest thing to a preview.
</InfoBox>

## The September decision

The next FOMC meeting is **September 15–16, 2026**, with the statement and press conference on the 16th. It's one of four meetings a year that ships a **Summary of Economic Projections** — the dot plot — so this isn't just a rate call, it's the Committee's first fresh look at where it sees 2026 and 2027 heading since the June meeting put 9 of 18 officials in hike territory with a median 2026 estimate of 3.8%.

Between now and then, the data that matters most is the August jobs report (due September 4, before the meeting) and any further inflation prints. As of today, prediction-market-style pricing has shifted toward easing after the cooler CPI print, though a hold remains a real possibility given how hawkish the June dot plot was:

## What a move actually does to your money

Whatever happens on September 16, here's the mechanical part that doesn't get enough attention.

  headers={["Your money", "What happens on a hold", "What happens on a cut"]}

    ["Fixed-rate mortgage you already have", "No change either way", "No change either way"],
    ["New mortgage shopping", "Tracks the 10-year Treasury, not the Fed directly", "Often moves before the decision, on expectations"],
  ]}
  highlightCol={1}

The line people skip past is the third one. Your existing fixed-rate mortgage does not reprice when the Fed moves — it's locked. New mortgage rates key off the 10-year Treasury, which trades on expectations and can move well before or even opposite to what the Fed actually does. If you're shopping for a mortgage right now, watch the bond market, not the FOMC calendar.

<InfoBox type="warning">
A single Jackson Hole sentence can swing markets for a day. That volatility is a trader's problem, not a saver's. If a plan changes because of one line from Wyoming, the plan was too fragile to begin with.
</InfoBox>

## Here's What I'd Actually Do

1. **Keep your emergency fund in a high-yield savings account regardless of the outcome.** It'll track whatever the Fed does with a short lag — you don't need to forecast the meeting to benefit from switching out of a 0.38%-APY big-bank account.
2. **If you believe a cut is coming and you're sitting on idle cash beyond your emergency fund, consider locking a CD or short T-bill now** to freeze today's yield before it potentially drifts lower. This is the one move that's genuinely time-sensitive.
3. **Don't touch a fixed-rate mortgage over this.** If you have an adjustable rate or are shopping for a new one, the 10-year Treasury tells you more than the Fed statement will.
4. **Read the September 16 dot plot, not the Jackson Hole hot takes.** The Committee's own updated projections carry more information than any single speech.
5. **Attack credit-card debt regardless of the outcome.** No plausible September move brings 20%+ APR down to a survivable level — see my breakdown of the [credit card and BNPL debt math](/posts/credit-card-bnpl-debt-2026).
