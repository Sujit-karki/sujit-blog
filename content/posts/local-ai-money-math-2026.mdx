## What I Tested

Eight arithmetic problems, each one something a person might actually
need answered: compound growth on a savings balance, a mortgage payment,
a percentage change, tax owed across three brackets, splitting a
restaurant bill, a portfolio allocation, an effective annual rate, and
whether an annual subscription beats a monthly one.

Every problem has one correct answer, computed by hand from a closed-form
formula and checked independently. Where a question could have depended
on current tax law, the brackets are supplied in the prompt itself — the
test is whether a model can do the arithmetic, not whether it has
memorised this year's tables.

Each model answered each question ten times, with a different random seed
each time. That is 80 generations per model and 320 in total.

<InfoBox type="tip">
The models were run entirely on my own machine through Ollama — an Acer
Nitro 5 with a GTX 1650 Ti, 4 GB of video memory and 8 GB of system RAM.
Nothing was sent to an API. Every model here is small enough to fit on
hardware most people already own, which is the entire point: this is the
tier a person actually runs at home.
</InfoBox>

## The Error That Should Worry You

`qwen2.5:3b` is the most accurate model here. This is its complete answer
to the tax question:

```text
To solve this, we need to calculate the tax on the taxable income of $50,000 using the given tax brackets.

1. **Calculate tax on the first bracket (0 to 11000):**
   - Income: $11,000
   - Tax rate: 10%
   - Tax amount: $11,000 * 0.10 = $1,100

2. **Calculate tax on the second bracket (11000 to 44725):**
   - Income: $33,725 ($44,725 - $11,000)
   - Tax rate: 12%
   - Tax amount: $33,725 * 0.12 = $3,947

3. **Calculate tax on the third bracket (44725 to 50000):**
   - Income: $50,000 - $44,725 = $5,275
   - Tax rate: 22%
   - Tax amount: $5,275 * 0.22 = $1,160.50

**Total tax owed:**
- Tax from first bracket: $1,100
- Tax from second bracket: $3,947
- Tax from third bracket: $1,160.50

**Total tax owed: $1,100 + $3,947 + $1,160.50 = $6,207.50**

ANSWER: 6207.50
```

That is the complete, unedited reply — asterisks and all.

Every structural decision is right. It identified all three brackets,
subtracted the boundaries correctly, and — the step most people get wrong
— stopped the top bracket at the taxpayer's actual $50,000 rather than
running it to the bracket ceiling of $95,375.

Then it computed `33,725 × 0.12` as `3,947`. The answer is `4,047`.

One multiplication, off by exactly $100, buried in the middle of working
that is otherwise flawless. The final figure of $6,207.50 arrives clean,
itemised and confident.

`llama3.2:3b` missed the identical multiplication in the other direction,
giving `4,045`. `gemma2:2b` failed differently and much more loudly — it
taxed the entire top bracket instead of stopping at $50,000, and returned
$19,888 against a true $6,307.50.

<InfoBox type="warning">
The three models produce errors of $100, $7.50 and $13,580 on the same
question. Only one of those is obvious enough that you would catch it by
glancing at the answer.
</InfoBox>

## You Cannot Catch These by Asking Twice

The obvious defence is to run the prompt again and see whether the answer
changes. It does not.

Across 32 model-question pairs, 31 returned an identical answer in all
ten repetitions — the same answer every time, whether right or wrong.
The single exception was `phi3.5:3.8b` on the mortgage question, which
split nine-to-one between $1,796.39 and $1,795.96. Both are wrong; the
true payment is $1,798.65.

To check this was not an artefact of one session, three of the four
models were run twice, hours apart, under very different system load. All
240 overlapping generations returned identical answers, and each model's
accuracy was unchanged.

These models are not guessing. They are reliably, repeatably wrong, which
removes the one safeguard a non-expert user might plausibly apply.

## Methodology

Every number in this post comes from a dataset I generated and published.
The harness, the raw generations and the aggregated results are in the
site's repository under `research/`, and the run is reproducible on any
machine with Ollama installed.

**Hardware.** Acer Nitro 5, NVIDIA GTX 1650 Ti (4 GB VRAM), 8 GB system
RAM, Windows 11. All four models ran fully on the GPU, confirmed via
`ollama ps`.

**Models.** `gemma2:2b`, `llama3.2:3b`, `qwen2.5:3b` and `phi3.5:3.8b`,
all at Q4_K_M quantisation — the default weights `ollama pull` fetches.
Ollama 0.33.2.

**Settings.** `temperature 0`, an explicit seed, `num_ctx 2048` and
`num_predict 1024` on every call. Temperature alone is not deterministic,
because GPU floating-point reduction order varies between runs, so all of
them are pinned. The first generation after a model loads behaves
differently from later ones, so one warm-up call per model was made and
discarded.

**A bug I found and fixed before publishing.** The first run used
`num_predict 512`. That silently cut off 20 generations — every one of
them `phi3.5:3.8b` on the mortgage and tax questions, the two it failed.
The replies ended on the bare word `ANSWER`, with the number itself lost,
which meant the most verbose model was being penalised for showing its
work. I raised the cap to 1024 and re-ran those 20. The answers came back
identical — 7450.50 on tax all ten times, and the same nine-to-one
mortgage split — so the scores here are unchanged, but they were briefly
resting on luck rather than on a sound measurement. The harness now
records Ollama's `done_reason` on every generation so a truncated reply
can never again be mistaken for a wrong answer.

**Repetitions.** Ten per question per model, each with a different seed
(42 through 51). A single sample cannot distinguish a model that is
reliably correct from one that is occasionally correct.

**Grading.** Prompts require a final `ANSWER: <number>` line; all 320
generations complied. Answers are compared against a hand-computed value
within a stated tolerance — one cent for exact arithmetic, wider where a
formula involves rounding a discount factor. Tolerances matter to the
result: `phi3.5:3.8b` answers the compound-interest question with
`16288.0` against a true `16288.95`, which is scored wrong here, and is
wrong, but only by 95 cents on a $16,289 balance.

**Ground truth.** Every expected value was computed by hand from a
closed-form formula and verified against an independent recomputation. No
model was consulted for a correct answer at any point.

**Disclosure.** The models in this experiment are the subject being
measured, not the author. The dataset was generated by running them; the
analysis above is written by me.
