Two changes landed in the same bill, and most coverage only mentions the flattering one. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act gives roughly 90% of filers — the ones who take the standard deduction — a brand-new charitable write-off starting this year. It also quietly makes itemized charitable deductions slightly less generous for people who already itemize. Both are true at once, and which one matters to you depends entirely on which side of the itemizing line you're on.

## The new break for the 90%

Under new **IRC §170(p)**, effective for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025, non-itemizers can now deduct **cash** gifts to qualifying 501(c)(3) public charities up to **$1,000 for single filers or $2,000 for married filing jointly** — as an above-the-line deduction that reduces your AGI even while you're still claiming the standard deduction. This is permanent law, not a temporary provision, and it's the first break of its kind since a similar pandemic-era rule expired.

The amounts are **not indexed for inflation**, so $1,000 and $2,000 will be worth a little less in real terms every year this stays in place without a legislative update — worth knowing so you're not surprised the number doesn't move with inflation the way brackets and other thresholds do.

## The quiet haircut for itemizers

Here's the part that gets far less coverage. If you itemize, OBBBA adds a **0.5%-of-AGI floor** to your charitable deduction — you subtract 0.5% of your AGI from your total giving before what's left becomes deductible. On $200,000 of AGI, that floor is $1,000; give $10,000 in cash and only $9,000 is actually deductible.

There's a second haircut layered on top for the highest earners: the tax **benefit of itemized deductions is capped at 35%** for anyone in the 37% marginal bracket, even though their bracket is higher. So a top-bracket donor doesn't get the full value of their marginal rate back on charitable gifts the way they would have before.

<InfoBox type="warning">
Put the two pieces together and the irony is real: OBBBA hands a new deduction to people who weren't itemizing, while modestly shrinking the deduction for people who were already giving generously and itemizing. Neither group should assume the headline "charitable deduction expanded" applies to them without checking which side of the line they're on.
</InfoBox>

## Why timing your gift matters this year

If you're near the standard-deduction threshold, this is a genuinely useful year to think about **bunching** — concentrating two or three years of planned giving into one tax year to clear the itemizing bar, then taking the standard deduction (plus the new $1,000/$2,000 non-itemizer break) in the off years. A donor-advised fund is the classic vehicle for this, though notably a DAF contribution itself doesn't qualify for the new non-itemizer break — more on that below.

The SALT cap increase I covered [in a separate piece](/posts/salt-deduction-cap-2026) interacts with this too: a higher SALT cap makes itemizing clear the standard-deduction bar more easily for homeowners in high-tax states, which can make a charitable bunching strategy pay off in a year it wouldn't have under the old $10,000 SALT cap.

## Cash only — and the DAF exclusion

Both halves of this rule share an important restriction: **cash only**. Appreciated stock, real estate, and other non-cash gifts don't qualify for either the new non-itemizer deduction or count toward the more favorable side of these particular rules. And **gifts to donor-advised funds and most private foundations are excluded** from the new $1,000/$2,000 above-the-line break specifically — it's aimed at direct gifts to operating public charities, not pass-through giving vehicles.

I'll name the obvious tension: this pushes small, spontaneous cash donors toward giving directly rather than through a DAF if they want to actually use the new deduction, even though DAFs remain a perfectly legitimate and often tax-efficient way to give for itemizers bunching larger gifts.

## Here's What I'd Actually Do

1. **If you don't itemize, start keeping cash-gift receipts again.** Up to $1,000 or $2,000 is now deductible even on the standard deduction — money that used to just disappear for most givers.
2. **If you itemize, run your numbers with the 0.5% floor included**, not the pre-2026 full deduction you might be used to claiming.
3. **Consider bunching if you're near the itemizing threshold**, especially if the higher SALT cap already moves you closer to itemizing this year.
4. **Give cash, or convert non-cash gifts to cash where it makes sense**, since the new non-itemizer break specifically requires cash.
5. **Give directly to the charity, not through a DAF, if the new $1,000/$2,000 deduction is the goal** — DAF gifts don't qualify for that specific break.

This isn't tax advice for your specific return — a CPA can tell you exactly where you land under both halves of this rule.
