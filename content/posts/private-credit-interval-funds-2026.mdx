Private credit funds are being marketed to retail investors harder than at any point I've watched this space, and the pitch usually leans on one word: liquidity. Quarterly redemptions, an evergreen structure, "semi-liquid" — language built to sound like a mutual fund. Then 2026 happened, and one of the largest funds in the category gated for the first time in its history. I want to walk through how these gates actually work, because the mechanism, not the marketing, is what determines whether you get your money when you ask for it.

## What these products actually are

An interval fund or a non-traded business development company (BDC) is a closed-end fund registered under the Investment Company Act of 1940. It doesn't trade on an exchange like an ETF. Instead, it periodically offers to repurchase a slice of shares — usually quarterly — so investors have *some* path to cash without needing a buyer on the other side of a trade. That's the entire liquidity mechanism: a scheduled, capped repurchase offer, not a market.

The category has grown fast. Private credit overall is roughly a **$2.6 trillion** global market, and semi-liquid retail vehicles — interval funds, tender-offer funds, non-traded BDCs — are the wrapper Wall Street has built to get part of that market in front of individual investors and 401(k)-adjacent channels.

## The liquidity is on the label, not guaranteed

Here's the mechanism that matters most and gets glossed over in the marketing: the SEC caps interval-fund repurchase offers, and most funds set their own quarterly ceiling at **5% of net asset value**. If redemption requests in a given quarter come in under that ceiling, everyone who asked gets paid. If requests come in *over* the ceiling, the fund prorates — each investor gets back only their share of the available 5%, and the rest of the request rolls to future quarters.

<InfoBox type="warning">
"Semi-liquid" describes the mechanism, not an outcome. In a calm quarter it can feel like a liquid fund. In a quarter where everyone wants out at once, it behaves like what it actually is: an illiquid portfolio with a capped, scheduled exit door.
</InfoBox>

## The 2026 stress test

This isn't theoretical anymore. In the first quarter of 2026, redemption requests at Blackstone's flagship **BCRED** fund ran close to **8% of NAV** — well above its standard 5% quarterly cap. Blackstone's board authorized upsizing the cap to 7%, and Blackstone plus senior executives' own capital covered the remaining gap, so every investor who asked to leave in Q1 2026 was actually paid in full. Other large managers saw even heavier pressure that quarter: Apollo's fund saw requests near 11.2% of NAV, Ares near 11.6%, and two Blue Owl vehicles saw 21.9% and 40.7% — figures that make BCRED's stress look mild by comparison.

The story changed in the second quarter. On **June 4, 2026**, Blackstone announced it would actually gate redemptions on BCRED — its roughly **$79 billion** fund — after investors submitted more than **10% of fund assets** in withdrawal requests. Blackstone capped redemptions at the standard 5% of NAV. It was the first time BCRED had ever hit its gate.

I want to be precise about what that means and doesn't mean. Nobody's principal disappeared. What happened is that investors who wanted out got roughly half of what they asked for that quarter, with the rest queued behind future repurchase offers — exactly the mechanism described in the fund's own prospectus, working as designed under real pressure.

<InfoBox type="info">
A gate is not a fund blowing up. It's the liquidity mechanism doing its job — protecting remaining investors from a fire sale of illiquid loans to fund an exit stampede. The real lesson is about expectations, not solvency.
</InfoBox>

## Fees, valuation, and what's really inside

Two other things I'd flag before anyone treats these as a bond substitute. First, fees run meaningfully higher than a public bond fund — management fees plus incentive fees that resemble hedge-fund or private-equity pricing more than fixed-income pricing. Second, the "net asset value" you see monthly is a manager-marked estimate of illiquid loans, not a market price. That's not necessarily wrong, but it means the NAV moves smoothly right up until a stress event forces real price discovery — which is part of why redemption queues can spike faster than the NAV itself signals trouble.

These are also generally taxed as ordinary income via 1099s rather than the K-1s associated with older private-fund structures, which is a genuine simplification for retail holders — worth naming as a real point in their favor, not just a caveat.

## Here's What I'd Actually Do

1. **Read the actual repurchase policy**, not the marketing page. Find the specific quarterly percentage cap and whether the manager has ever gated.
2. **Size the position assuming a delayed exit.** Treat any private-credit allocation as money you might not fully access for several quarters if things get bumpy — not as a cash-equivalent.
3. **Check whether the fund has already gated.** BCRED's June 2026 gate is public information; ask any fund you're considering for its redemption history, not just its target yield.
4. **Compare the fee load to what you're actually getting.** Higher yield than public bonds should come with meaningfully more analysis than "it pays more."
5. **Don't confuse quarterly redemptions with daily liquidity**, even when a platform's app makes buying and "requesting" a withdrawal feel equally instant.

Not investment advice — private credit carries real illiquidity and credit risk regardless of how the product is packaged.
