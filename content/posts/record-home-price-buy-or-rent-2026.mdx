The housing headlines in July 2026 are contradictory on purpose: "record prices" and "improving affordability" ran in the same week. Both are true, and the gap between them is where your decision actually lives. Let me get past the macro to the one question that matters — should *you* buy right now?

Per NAR's July report, the median existing-home price rose to a record **$440,600, up 1.8% from a year earlier — the 36th consecutive month of annual price appreciation** — even as the pace of sales actually fell 2.4% from May, though it's still up 2.8% from June 2025. That 1.8% annual gain is far below the double-digit pandemic-era pace: prices are grinding up, not exploding.

## Why it feels worse than the numbers

Here's a place two statistics genuinely diverge. NAR's chief economist has pointed out that affordability is actually *better* than a year ago because wage growth is outpacing home-price growth — yet several affordability trackers have shown conditions slipping in recent months, largely because the Iran-war energy spike pushed mortgage rates back up from a dip earlier in the year.

One caveat about that headline price worth carrying into any comparison: a
median is not a like-for-like price index. It measures the middle of whatever
happened to sell that month, so when high-end sales hold up better than
entry-level ones — which is exactly what happens as rates squeeze first-time
buyers out — the median rises even if no individual house gained a dollar. A
record median can partly be a record *mix*.

That is why the Census Bureau's new-home price series and repeat-sales indices
often tell a slightly different story from the existing-home median in the same
month. If you are trying to work out whether the specific house you want has got
more expensive, the national median is close to the least useful number
available. Local inventory and days-on-market tell you more about your actual
negotiating position than any national figure will.

## The real buy-vs-rent question

Timing the market is a losing game. The decision that actually matters is **how long you'll stay**, because buying carries big up-front costs — closing costs typically run 2%–5% of the loan — that only amortize over years.

### The five-year rule is a 4%-mortgage artifact

"Buy if you'll stay five years" gets repeated everywhere without conditions. I built the actual month-by-month comparison — two households, same money, one buying and one renting-and-investing-the-difference — and ran the break-even at this year's price and rate. [The script is published](https://github.com/Sujit-karki/sujit-blog/blob/main/scripts/buy-vs-rent.mjs).

The renter starts with the down payment and closing costs still invested, and invests the gap whenever the owner's monthly outlay is higher. The buyer pays interest, principal, property tax, insurance and maintenance, and sells at the horizon with 6% selling costs. Break-even is the first month the buyer's net worth overtakes the renter's.

| Mortgage rate | Monthly P&I | Break-even |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 4% | $1,683 | **6.8 years** |
| 5% | $1,892 | 16.2 years |
| 5.5% | $2,001 | never |
| 6.55% | $2,240 | **never** |
| 7.5% | $2,465 | never |

At $440,600 against $2,100 rent and 3% appreciation, buying **never breaks even inside thirty years** at today's rate. The familiar five-to-seven-year answer reappears at 4% and nowhere above 5%. It was never a property of housing — it was a property of the rate environment the advice was written in.

### What actually moves it

Not the holding period. Rent and appreciation:

| Appreciation | Rent $1,800 | Rent $2,100 | Rent $2,400 | Rent $2,800 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0% | never | never | 28.8 yr | 16.5 yr |
| 3% | never | never | 17.1 yr | **6.0 yr** |
| 5% | never | 8.4 yr | 4.5 yr | 2.9 yr |

Read the columns, not the rows. Moving rent from $2,100 to $2,800 does more than any plausible change in how long you stay. **The buy-or-rent question at this rate is really a question about what renting costs you locally** — the national price and the national rate barely enter it.

Two honest asymmetries, since they cut opposite ways: I model no mortgage-interest deduction, because after the OBBBA standard deduction most buyers do not itemise — that understates buying for those who do. And I apply no capital-gains tax to the renter's portfolio, which flatters renting by a similar order. Neither is netted silently; both are in the dataset.

## Here's What I'd Actually Do

1. **Run your break-even before you tour a single house.** If your horizon is under ~5 years, lean rent.
2. **Use rising inventory as leverage.** A 4.6-month supply gives buyers more negotiating room than in the frenzy years — ask for concessions.
3. **Don't wait for a magic rate.** At 6.55% you can refinance later if rates fall; you can't un-overpay for a house bought in a bidding war.
4. **Protect your emergency fund.** Don't drain savings for a bigger down payment and leave yourself exposed — especially with energy prices volatile right now.

## Methodology

**The comparison.** Two households, same money, month by month for thirty years. The buyer's down payment and closing costs leave the portfolio; each month splits into interest and principal, with property tax (1.1%), insurance (0.5%) and maintenance (1.0%) paid on the home's value on top; at the horizon the house sells with 6% selling costs and the mortgage is retired. The renter keeps the down payment and closing costs invested at 7%, pays rent growing at 3.5%, and invests the difference whenever the owner's monthly outlay is higher. Break-even is the first month the buyer's net worth overtakes the renter's.

Every dollar is counted once: the interest, tax, maintenance and transaction costs are real money leaving the buyer, and the foregone return on the down payment is real money the renter keeps earning. Comparisons that drop either side are why the published answer varies so wildly.

**Verification.** Three checks, any of which stops the run: the degenerate scenario must still produce a finite number, a higher mortgage rate must never *shorten* the break-even, and faster appreciation must never *lengthen* it. Both monotonicity checks are properties the model must have, so a violation means the loop is wrong rather than the world being surprising.

**The two asymmetries, stated rather than netted.** No mortgage-interest or property-tax deduction, because after the OBBBA standard deduction most buyers do not itemise — this understates buying for the minority who do. And no capital-gains tax on the renter's portfolio, which flatters renting by roughly the same order. They push in opposite directions and both are in the dataset.

**Not modelled.** The value of security of tenure, and the cost of being unable to move.
