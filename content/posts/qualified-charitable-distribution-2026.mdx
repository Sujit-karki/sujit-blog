If you're taking required minimum distributions and giving to charity anyway, there's a decent chance you're doing it in the more expensive order. Most people take the RMD as taxable income, then write a check to charity and try to deduct it. A qualified charitable distribution skips that whole detour — the money goes straight from your IRA to the charity and never shows up in your income in the first place. Here's why that ordering matters more than it sounds like it should.

## The RMD basics and the deadline

Required minimum distributions kick in at **age 73** for people born 1951-1959, or **age 75** for people born 1960 or later, under SECURE 2.0's phased-in schedule. The deadline for taking your RMD each year is **December 31** — except for your very first RMD, which you can delay until April 1 of the following year (though doing so means taking two RMDs in one calendar year, which can push you into a higher bracket).

Miss the deadline and the penalty is real: a **25% excise tax** on the amount you should have withdrawn but didn't, reduced to **10% if you correct it within two years**. That's steep enough that "I'll get to it in December" is a genuinely risky plan if your custodian's systems get backed up during the year-end rush.

## Why the QCD beats a deduction now

A qualified charitable distribution lets IRA owners **age 70½ or older** send money directly from their IRA to a qualifying charity — up to **$111,000 per person in 2026** — and that amount counts toward satisfying your RMD for the year. The critical difference from writing a check yourself: the QCD amount is **excluded from your AGI entirely**. It never appears as income you then try to deduct back out.

That distinction matters more than it looks like on paper. If you take the RMD as income and then deduct an equivalent charitable gift, you need to itemize to get any benefit at all — and even then, the new 0.5%-of-AGI floor on itemized giving I wrote about [in a separate piece on the 2026 charitable deduction changes](/posts/charitable-deduction-changes-2026) shaves a little off the deduction. A QCD sidesteps all of that. You get the full benefit whether or not you itemize, because the income never counted in the first place.

<InfoBox type="tip">
Married couples can each use their own $111,000 QCD limit from their own IRAs — up to $222,000 combined in 2026 — which matters for larger charitable commitments in a single year.
</InfoBox>

## The AGI-exclusion ripple effect

Because a QCD keeps money out of AGI rather than adding it and deducting it back out, it helps with things a deduction can't touch. A lower AGI can mean lower **Medicare IRMAA surcharges** the following year — I've written separately about [how income affects your Medicare premium through IRMAA](/posts/medicare-2027-part-b-open-enrollment) — less of your **Social Security benefit** subject to tax, and a lower exposure to the **net investment income tax**. A deduction reduces your taxable income; a QCD keeps the income from ever counting toward any of these AGI-based thresholds in the first place. For a retiree sitting right at one of those cliffs, that difference can be worth more than the deduction's face value.

## The rules people get wrong

A few mechanics trip people up every year. The distribution must go **directly from the IRA custodian to the charity** — if the money touches your hands first, even briefly, it's disqualified as a QCD and becomes an ordinary taxable distribution. **Donor-advised funds and most private foundations don't qualify** as QCD recipients; it has to be a direct-giving qualifying charity. And for **inherited IRAs**, the beneficiary can use a QCD only if they themselves are 70½ or older — the original owner's age doesn't transfer.

<InfoBox type="warning">
Ask your custodian for their specific QCD process well before December. Some require a separate form or a distinct checkbook tied to the IRA, and a QCD requested too close to year-end can miss the December 31 deadline through no fault of your own.
</InfoBox>

## Here's What I'd Actually Do

1. **If you're 70½+ and charitably inclined, default to QCDs over writing checks from taxable accounts** once RMDs are in play — the AGI exclusion is close to strictly better.
2. **Start your QCD paperwork in October or November**, not December — custodian processing times vary and the December 31 deadline is firm.
3. **Coordinate with your itemizing status.** If you don't itemize, a QCD is even more clearly the better move than a deduction you can't actually use.
4. **Watch your combined RMD + other income against IRMAA and Social Security taxation thresholds** — the QCD's real value often shows up there, not just on the tax return line.
5. **Confirm your specific charity qualifies** and that the distribution goes directly from custodian to charity — don't let the check route through you first.

This isn't tax advice for your specific return — a CPA or fee-only advisor can model your exact numbers against your other income sources.
