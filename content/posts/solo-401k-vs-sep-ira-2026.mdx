If you earn 1099 income — freelancing, consulting, a real side hustle — the retirement account you pick can change your tax bill by thousands of dollars. The two workhorses are the Solo 401(k) and the SEP IRA. They land at the same $72,000 ceiling in 2026, which makes people assume they're interchangeable. They aren't. The difference is how you climb to that ceiling, and on modest income it isn't close.

## Same ceiling, different ladder

For 2026 both plans max out at **$72,000** — that's the section 415(c) annual-addition limit. But they get there differently.

A SEP IRA is funded entirely by the "employer" (you). The limit is 25% of compensation, which for a sole proprietor works out to roughly **20% of net self-employment earnings** after you back out the deduction for half your self-employment tax. No employee piece, no catch-up.

A Solo 401(k) has two buckets: an **employee deferral of up to $24,500** in 2026, plus employer profit-sharing of up to 25% of compensation, up to the same $72,000 combined. And it allows catch-up contributions: **$8,000 at 50+**, or an enhanced **$11,250 for anyone who turns 60, 61, 62, or 63 in 2026**.

## Why income level decides it

Both plans are defined entirely by statute, so this isn't a matter of judgement — the crossover is a closed-form consequence of four IRS figures and can be stated to the dollar. I computed the whole curve rather than quoting a round number for it ([the script is published](https://github.com/Sujit-karki/sujit-blog/blob/main/scripts/self-employed-plans.mjs), as is the dataset below).

| Net profit | SEP IRA max | Solo 401(k) max | Solo advantage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $40,000 | $7,435 | $31,935 | **$24,500** |
| $60,000 | $11,152 | $35,652 | **$24,500** |
| $100,000 | $18,587 | $43,087 | **$24,500** |
| $150,000 | $27,881 | $52,381 | **$24,500** |
| $200,000 | $37,177 | $61,677 | **$24,500** |
| $250,000 | $47,043 | $71,543 | **$24,500** |
| $275,000 | $51,976 | $72,000 | $20,024 |
| $300,000 | $56,909 | $72,000 | $15,091 |
| $350,000 | $66,775 | $72,000 | $5,225 |
| $400,000 | $72,000 | $72,000 | $0 |

Two things fall out of that table that the usual write-up misses.

**The advantage is flat, not shrinking.** From about $40,000 of profit — the point where your net earnings can actually absorb the full deferral — all the way to $252,316, the Solo 401(k) lets you contribute exactly **$24,500 more**. Not proportionally more. The same $24,500, every year, whether you profit $50,000 or $250,000. It is simply the employee deferral, which the SEP has no equivalent of.

**The convergence point is much higher than usually quoted.** A Solo 401(k) reaches the $72,000 ceiling at **$252,316** of net profit. A SEP IRA does not get there until **$376,478** — it needs **$124,162 more profit** to arrive at the identical ceiling. Anywhere below that, the SEP is leaving room on the table.

<InfoBox type="tip" title="Why $360,000 is the number that ends it">
The SEP catches up at exactly $360,000 of *net earnings*, and that is not a coincidence. $360,000 is the section 401(a)(17) compensation limit for 2026, and 20% of it is $72,000 — precisely the section 415(c) cap. Two limits set by different statutes and adjusted separately happen to be calibrated so that a SEP's maximum lands on the overall maximum at exactly the point where compensation stops counting. The script checks that identity still holds each year and says so loudly if a future adjustment breaks it.
</InfoBox>

Below the crossover the Solo 401(k) is unambiguously more generous; above it the two are identical and the SEP's simpler paperwork is a real advantage for no cost in contribution room.

<InfoBox type="tip">
If you have a W-2 job with a 401(k) and a side hustle, your $24,500 employee deferral limit is shared across all 401(k)s — you don't get a fresh $24,500 in the Solo plan. But the employer profit-sharing side is separate for each plan, so a Solo 401(k) can still add real room on top of what you're already deferring at your day job.
</InfoBox>

## The deadlines that trip people up

This is where people lose money by accident. To make **2026 contributions to a Solo 401(k), the plan must be established by December 31, 2026.** Miss that date and the door is closed for the year, even though you can still fund the account you already opened after year-end.

A **SEP IRA can be opened and funded up to your tax-filing deadline, including extensions** — as late as October 2027 for a 2026 contribution if you file an extension. So if it's December and you don't have a plan yet, the Solo 401(k) is your last call; if it's spring and you're scrambling, the SEP is your rescue plan.

## Roth, loans, and paperwork

The Solo 401(k) usually offers a **Roth option** and permits **plan loans**, typically up to $50,000. The SEP IRA has no Roth option at most custodians and no loan provision. On administration, the SEP wins: one form, no annual filing until assets hit $250,000. A Solo 401(k) is more paperwork and requires filing Form 5500-EZ once assets cross that same $250,000 threshold.

I'll name the counterargument: if you might hire employees beyond a spouse, the Solo 401(k) stops being available, and a SEP (or a different plan entirely) becomes the practical choice. Pick for where your business is going, not just where it is today.

If you're weighing this alongside other tax moves on self-employment income, I've also written about [the Roth catch-up rule that now hits high W-2 earners](/posts/mandatory-roth-catch-up-2026) and [where the 2027 contribution limits are shaping up](/posts/401k-ira-contribution-limits-2027) — both useful context if you're straddling a day job and a side business.

## See which plan lets you save more

**How I calculated this:** SEP-IRA max = 25% of net self-employment earnings (after the deduction for half your self-employment tax), capped at the $72,000 2026 limit. Solo 401(k) max = your employee elective deferral ($24,500, or $32,500 with the 50+ catch-up) *plus* the same 25%-of-net-earnings employer contribution, combined still capped at $72,000 — the gap between the two is that employee-deferral piece, which is why a solo 401(k) usually wins at lower income and the two converge as income rises. ## Methodology

**The arithmetic**, from IRS Publication 560's deduction worksheet. Net earnings from self-employment are net profit less one-half of the deductible SE tax. The employer contribution rate is not 25% but `0.25 / 1.25 = 0.20`, because a sole proprietor's own compensation is itself reduced by the contribution — this is the step most write-ups skip, and it is why a SEP on $100,000 of profit funds $18,587 rather than $25,000. Net earnings are then capped at the section 401(a)(17) compensation limit, and both plans' totals at the section 415(c) limit.

**SE tax** is 15.3% on 92.35% of net profit, with the 12.4% Social Security half stopping at the $184,500 wage base and the 2.9% Medicare half uncapped. The additional 0.9% Medicare tax above $200,000 is included in SE tax but excluded from the one-half deduction, per section 164(f).

**The four statutory figures**, each quoted from an IRS page rather than a secondary source: elective deferral $24,500, section 415(c) annual additions $72,000, section 401(a)(17) compensation limit $360,000, Social Security wage base $184,500. All four are linked in the sources below.

**Verification.** The script recomputes SE tax at $100,000 against a hand-derived figure and refuses to publish if they disagree. It also asserts the calibration described above — that 20% of the compensation limit equals the 415(c) cap exactly — and reports loudly rather than silently if a future year's adjustments break it, because the entire "$360,000 ends it" framing depends on that identity holding.

**What this does not model.** A sole proprietor on Schedule C, under 50, with no employees. An S-corp owner's base is W-2 wages rather than net profit, which changes everything. Catch-up contributions raise both ceilings and move both crossovers. State tax, the QBI deduction, and hiring anyone beyond a spouse all affect the decision without affecting these ceilings.

## Here's What I'd Actually Do

1. **Estimate your 2026 net self-employment income.** That single number drives the decision more than anything else here.
2. **If it's under $252,316 and you want to save aggressively, open a Solo 401(k)** — before December 31, 2026. Below that figure the SEP cannot reach the same ceiling no matter what you do.
3. **If you're time-crunched or want zero paperwork, use a SEP IRA** and take advantage of the later setup deadline.
4. **Decide Roth vs. pre-tax deliberately.** If you expect higher future tax rates, the Solo 401(k)'s Roth bucket is worth having even if it's not required.
5. **If you have a day-job 401(k), coordinate the $24,500 deferral** across both plans so you don't accidentally over-contribute.

Not financial advice — talk to a CPA about your entity type before you fund anything.
