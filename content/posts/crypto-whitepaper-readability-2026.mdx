"Do your own research" is the standing advice in crypto, and the whitepaper is
where you are told to start. Every project has one. It is presented as the
document that lets an ordinary person evaluate the thing before putting money in.

So: can an ordinary person read it? That is a measurable question, and the tools
for measuring it are older than the internet.

## The measurement

Flesch Reading Ease and Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level are the formulas schools,
insurers and government plain-language rules have used for decades. They are
arithmetic over three counts — sentences, words, syllables:

> **Reading Ease** = 206.835 − 1.015 × (words per sentence) − 84.6 × (syllables per word)
> Higher is easier. 60–70 is considered plain English.

No judgement, no vocabulary list, no model deciding what counts as clear. Anyone
with the same text gets the same number to the decimal.

I ran five whitepapers through it, alongside four documents whose difficulty you
already have an intuition for, so the scores land on a scale rather than floating
free.

**Every whitepaper measured is harder to read than IRS Publication 17.**

That is worth sitting with. Publication 17 is the general guide to filing a
federal tax return — the document people invoke as shorthand for unreadable
government writing. It scores 49.7. Bitcoin's whitepaper, the most readable of
the five, scores 47.5.

The best crypto whitepaper is harder going than the tax instructions.

Bitcoin's is the plainest of the group and still asks for a **high-school-senior**
reading level. Ethereum's asks for a **college junior**. Cardano's Ouroboros
paper has **27.4% complex words** — more than one word in four with three or more
syllables, the densest in the entire set including the Constitution.

<InfoBox type="tip">
Notice which document is easiest after the children's book: the **FTC's guide to
crypto scams**, at 53.4. The clearest writing about crypto in this sample comes
from the agency warning people about it, not from the projects asking for their
money. That is not an accident — plain-language standards are a legal
requirement for federal consumer material.
</InfoBox>

## Why this matters more than it sounds

The stock answer is that whitepapers are technical documents and technical
documents are hard. Fair enough for a cryptography paper aimed at cryptographers.

But that is not the role the whitepaper plays. It is cited to retail buyers as
the thing that makes an investment legible — *read the whitepaper* is offered as
the answer to *how do I know this is real*. A document requiring a college
reading level cannot serve that function for most people, and roughly half of US
adults read below the level Ethereum's whitepaper demands.

The practical consequence is that "read the whitepaper" functions less as advice
than as a filter. It sounds like due diligence and mostly produces the appearance
of it — people skim, recognise a few words, and conclude the project is
substantial because the document was hard. Difficulty reads as rigour when you
cannot evaluate the content.

If you cannot get through the whitepaper, that is not a personal failing and it
is not a reason to buy anyway. It is a reason to treat the "do your own research"
framing with suspicion, because the research it points at is not written for you.

## What the formulas cannot see

Three limits, and the third is the one that matters most.

**Sample size.** Five whitepapers, not five hundred. These are among the
best-known projects with stable, publicly fetchable documents. A broader sample
would likely be worse, since obscure projects have less editorial attention, but
that is a guess and this does not test it.

**One document failed.** Litecoin's site did not yield enough continuous prose to
score — its landing page is mostly navigation. It is reported as a failure in the
data rather than quietly dropped, because a missing row that goes unmentioned is
how samples get flattering.

**The formulas measure length, not difficulty.** This is the real limitation.
Flesch counts syllables and sentence length; it has no idea what an elliptic
curve is. A paper of short sentences about hard mathematics scores as easy, and a
paper of long sentences about nothing scores as hard. The US Constitution's 23.8
is largely an artefact of 18th-century sentence construction, not conceptual
density — which is exactly why it is in the table, as a visible reminder of what
these numbers can and cannot tell you.

So read the results as a **floor**. The whitepapers are at least this hard. On
conceptual difficulty — the thing a reader actually runs into — they are
certainly harder, and no formula in this post can tell you by how much.

## Methodology

The script and the full results are in this site's repository under `research/`,
and the analysis re-runs against live documents.

**Documents.** Five whitepapers (Bitcoin, Ethereum, Solana, Ripple, Cardano's
Ouroboros) fetched from each project's own domain, plus four references: the US
Constitution, IRS Publication 17, the FTC's crypto scam guide, and *Alice in
Wonderland*.

**Extraction.** PDFs parsed with `pypdf`; HTML stripped of script, style and
navigation before counting. URLs, citation markers and non-ASCII artefacts are
removed, since they distort word length without being words anyone reads.

**Formulas.** Flesch Reading Ease and Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level as stated above.
Syllables counted by the standard vowel-group heuristic with a silent-final-e
adjustment — approximate, applied identically to every document, so comparisons
between them hold even where an absolute count is off by a little.

**Why formulas rather than a model.** [The previous experiment on this
site](/posts/ai-advice-benchmark-that-failed-2026) tried to score qualitative
text by keyword matching and the metric was wrong in both directions. Readability
indices are deterministic arithmetic with no vocabulary list and no judgement,
which is the right instrument for a question like this one.
