If you or a parent lives on Social Security, the number that matters most each year isn't the stock market — it's the cost-of-living adjustment. This post originally ran in July with the forecasters' estimates. July's actual inflation data has since been published, so I have replaced the forecast with the arithmetic.

## What the actual data says

The COLA is not a forecast, a negotiation, or a policy choice. Since 1975 it has
been one piece of arithmetic: take the average CPI-W for **July, August and
September**, compare it with the same three months a year earlier, round to the
nearest tenth of a percent. That is the entire rule.

So rather than repeat someone else's estimate, here is that formula run against
the published series — CPI-W, BLS series `CWUR0000SA0`.

**First, does the method work?** Before trusting any projection, it should
reproduce a COLA the SSA has already announced:

It matches exactly. That is the only reason the next table is worth reading.

**Now 2027.** The comparison basis is the Q3 2025 average of **317.2647**. Of the
three months that decide it, only July 2026 has been published: **327.104**.
August and September are still to come.

If August and September simply hold flat at July's level, the 2027 COLA comes to
**3.1%**. Reaching the 3.8% the summer forecasts pointed at would need both
months to run about 0.65% hotter, every month — a real possibility, but not the
base case the published data supports.

<InfoBox type="warning">
Notice how wide that spread is: a plausible range of monthly outcomes moves the
COLA a full point, from 2.7% to 3.7%. That is not sloppiness in the estimates —
it is arithmetic. The COLA averages **three** months, so each unpublished month
carries a third of the weight. Compare that with the [2027 tax
brackets](/posts/2027-tax-brackets-projected), which average twelve months: there,
the same uncertainty in one month moves the answer by less than a tenth of a
percent. Same kind of formula, completely different sensitivity — and it is why
COLA predictions lurch around all summer while bracket projections sit still.
</InfoBox>

The script that produces these numbers is in this site's repository and re-runs
against live BLS data, so this section updates as August and September land
rather than going stale.

### Why this number carries so much weight

This isn't a rounding error for most recipients. Per Congressional Research Service data cited by AARP and SSA reliance studies, most beneficiaries lean on Social Security for a large share of their household income:

  title="How much of retirees' income is Social Security (age 65+)"

Three in five beneficiaries get *at least half* their income from that check, and roughly one in seven get nearly all of it. That's why the full-point spread still on the table — 2.7% vs. 3.7% — isn't academic: for the 90%+ group in particular, it's close to their entire raise for the year, full stop.

## The Medicare Part B catch

Here's the part the headline number hides. For most retirees, the Medicare Part B premium is deducted straight from the Social Security check. The 2026 Medicare Trustees Report projects the standard 2027 Part B premium at **around $209.50/month**, up from $202.90 in 2026 — a projection, not a final number. CMS doesn't announce the actual 2027 premium until around November 2026, and past projections have missed (the 2022 premium, for example, was projected at $158.50 but finalized at $170.10).

<InfoBox type="warning">
Don't budget the full 3.1%. Budget the raise *minus* your expected Medicare premium increase. For lower benefit amounts, the Part B bump can eat a meaningful slice of the COLA — and the real number won't be locked in until November.
</InfoBox>

## The uncomfortable trade-off

A larger COLA helps recipients now but strains the system. The nonpartisan **Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget (CRFB)** estimated in May that a 3.8% COLA in 2027 would add roughly **$300 billion** to Social Security's fiscal shortfall over the next decade and advance a key trust fund's projected depletion date by about three months, from late 2032. CRFB separately estimates that hitting that insolvency date without Congressional action would force an automatic ~25% across-the-board benefit cut. That's not a reason to root against your own raise — but it's the honest context.

## Here's What I'd Actually Do

1. **Treat the estimate as a range, not a promise.** Plan around 3.1%, treat 2.7% as the downside, and treat anything above 3.5% as upside. The official number is October.
2. **Net out Medicare.** Calculate raise minus expected Part B increase for your real spendable change — and don't assume $209.50 is locked in.
3. **If you're not yet claiming, don't let COLA math rush you.** Delaying benefits still raises your base far more than any single year's COLA.
4. **Revisit your withdrawal rate.** A higher COLA slightly reduces how much you must pull from savings — recalculate once the official number lands.
