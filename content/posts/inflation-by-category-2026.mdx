Every month a single number gets reported as the inflation rate, and every month
a large number of people read it and think it is wrong, because it does not match
what happened to their money.

They are not imagining it. The headline number is an average, and the things it
averages are moving at wildly different speeds — not by a little, and not
occasionally.

## One report, twelve answers

These figures are all from the same Bureau of Labor Statistics release, the same
index basis, the same month:

## Why the average describes nobody

The headline rate weights each category by how much a statistically average
household spends on it. That is the right way to build a national index. It is
also why the result matches almost no individual.

Consider two people in the same city in the same month:

- One drives to work, flies to see family twice a year, and rents. Their basket
  leans on gasoline (24.6%), airline fares (25.6%) and shelter (3.2%). Their lived
  inflation is well into double digits.
- One works from home, owns outright, and spends heavily on healthcare and a
  child's education. Their basket leans on medical care (1.7%) and education
  (0.55%). Their lived inflation is close to nothing.

Both read "inflation is 3.4%" and one of them concludes the statistics are lying.
Neither is wrong about their own experience. **Seven of the eleven component
categories here sit below the headline** — the average is dragged upward by a
small number of fast movers, which is exactly the shape that makes a mean
unrepresentative.

<InfoBox type="tip">
This is also why "inflation is coming down" and "everything is still expensive"
are both true at once. A falling *rate* means prices are rising more slowly, not
that they are falling. Cumulative increases stay in the price level, and in
categories like airline fares they compound from an already elevated base.
</InfoBox>

## Work out your own rate

You do not need a model for this, just an honest look at where your money goes.

1. **Take your last three months of spending** and split it into these
   categories. Rough percentages are fine — this is not accounting.
2. **Multiply each share by that category's rate** from the chart above.
3. **Add them up.** That is your personal inflation rate, and it is the number
   your budget actually experiences.

Someone spending 20% on transport and fuel, 30% on shelter and 15% on food gets
a very different answer from someone spending 5%, 45% and 10%. The exercise takes
ten minutes and is more useful than any headline, because it tells you which of
your own line items is doing the damage.

The practical follow-on: **the fast-moving categories are where substitution
actually pays.** A 25% rise in airline fares is worth restructuring travel around.
A 1.7% rise in medical care is not worth reorganising your life over, however
much the total bill stings.

## What this does not mean

Three honest limits, because this argument gets misused in both directions.

**It does not mean the official figure is wrong or manipulated.** It is a
correctly constructed weighted average, published transparently with every
component available for anyone to check — which is exactly how this post was
written. The problem is in how a single number gets reported, not in how it is
computed.

**It does not mean your rate is necessarily higher.** The categories running
hottest here are energy and travel. If you neither drive nor fly much, your
personal rate this year is likely *below* 3.36%, not above it. The spread cuts
both ways and people tend to assume only one direction.

**Category rates are not stable.** Energy is the most volatile component in the
index and its 14.7% could be materially different next month. A personal rate
computed today is a snapshot, not a trend.

## Methodology

Every figure comes from the BLS public API. The computed table is published with
this post as [cpi-categories.json](/data/cpi-categories.json); the script that
fetches and computes it is in this site's repository, which is not public yet.

**Data.** CPI-U, US city average, not seasonally adjusted — the same basis the
headline figure is published on, so the comparison is like for like. Twelve
series: all items, plus eleven components. Twelve-month change is the latest
published month against the same month a year earlier.

**Period.** Latest available at time of writing is July 2026.

**Reproducing it.** `npm run cpi:categories` in the repository re-fetches from
BLS and prints the current table. The figures above will age; the command will
not.

**A gap worth knowing about.** October 2025 is missing from the published CPI
series entirely — the data jumps from September to November that year. It does
not affect the 12-month changes reported here, which compare July to July, but it
is a real hole in the record and it appears across the whole CPI family.
