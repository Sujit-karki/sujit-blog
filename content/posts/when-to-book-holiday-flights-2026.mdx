Every fall the same two pieces of advice circulate at the same time: "book now before prices spike" and "wait for a sale, prices always drop." Both can't be right for the same trip, and mostly they aren't — they're describing different holidays. Let me walk through what the actual fare data shows for Thanksgiving and the December holidays, because the right move genuinely differs between them.

## The booking window, by holiday

Thanksgiving 2026 falls on **Thursday, November 26**. Fare-tracking data consistently shows Thanksgiving airfare bottoming out when booked **mid-to-late October** — roughly four to six weeks out — after which prices climb steadily into the holiday itself.

Christmas and New Year's travel behaves differently because the travel window is longer and less rigid — people fly out anywhere from mid-December through early January. Fares for that stretch are generally cheapest when booked **by the end of October**, and pricing studies find Christmas fares specifically tend to bottom **32 to 73 days out** from a given travel date, a noticeably wider window than Thanksgiving's tighter spike.

<InfoBox type="tip">
The practical takeaway: if you're doing both trips, book Thanksgiving first. Its window closes faster and the penalty for waiting is steeper on a percentage basis than Christmas.
</InfoBox>

## Cheapest days to fly

Day of the week still matters as much as booking lead time. Flying **Friday instead of Sunday** can save up to roughly **8%** on the same route, and flying **Tuesday** can run about **14% cheaper than Sunday**. Sunday is consistently the most expensive day around any holiday period because it's the default "back to normal life" travel day for the largest share of travelers.

If your dates have any flexibility at all — even shifting a Thanksgiving return from Sunday to Saturday or Monday — that single change often moves the needle more than booking a few weeks earlier does.

## Why 'wait for a sale' fails at Thanksgiving

Here's the part that trips people up. Airlines run frequent flash sales throughout the fall, and it's tempting to assume patience pays off. It doesn't for Thanksgiving specifically: **Thanksgiving travel dates are almost never included in promotional fare sales**, because demand on those specific days is already inelastic — the airline doesn't need to discount seats that are going to sell anyway. Waiting for a sale on a Thanksgiving-week flight is, in practice, waiting for a discount that isn't coming while the base fare keeps climbing toward the holiday.

Christmas is a little more forgiving because the travel window is wider and less concentrated on a handful of specific dates, so there's more genuine variation in pricing to catch if you watch closely. But even there, "wait and see" is a weaker strategy than booking inside the proven window once you know your dates.

For the bigger-picture context on why any of this year's fares are running where they are, I've also written about [why flights are so expensive in 2026 overall](/posts/why-are-flights-so-expensive-2026) — worth reading if the base fares on your route already look high before you even factor in holiday timing.

## International is a different game

Everything above is calibrated to domestic US routes. International holiday travel — especially anywhere popular for a winter break — typically needs a considerably longer lead time, often **two to three months or more**, because seat inventory on long-haul routes is smaller relative to demand and doesn't refill the way domestic capacity does. If you're leaving the country for the holidays, don't apply the October domestic windows above; you likely needed to book already, and the next-best move is to book now rather than wait for either window described here.

## Here's What I'd Actually Do

1. **Book Thanksgiving by mid-to-late October**, full stop — don't wait for a sale that historically doesn't include those dates.
2. **Book Christmas/New Year's by the end of October** if your dates are fixed; if they're flexible, you have a bit more room to watch prices into November.
3. **Shift off Sunday if you can.** Friday or Tuesday departures are the single easiest lever most travelers ignore.
4. **If you're flying internationally, don't use the domestic windows above** — book as soon as your dates are set.
5. **Set a price-tracking alert now** on your specific route so you have a real baseline instead of guessing whether a fare you see in October is actually good.
