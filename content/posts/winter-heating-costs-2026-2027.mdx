## What actually happened

The Energy Information Administration's Winter Fuels Outlook is the closest thing to an official seasonal forecast for household heating costs, built from fuel price projections and typical usage patterns by region. Its most recent edition (October 15, 2025, covering the 2025-26 winter) painted a genuinely mixed picture rather than a single "costs are up" or "costs are down" headline: homes primarily heated by natural gas were projected to spend about the same as the prior winter, electricity-heated homes were projected to pay more, and propane and heating-oil homes were projected to pay less.

That divergence is the actual story, and it's why a single national "average winter heating bill" number is close to useless for planning your own budget — it blends four fuels moving in different directions into one figure that doesn't describe any specific household. Natural gas remains the most common home heating fuel at roughly 46% of US households, followed by electricity at about 43%; propane and heating oil split the remaining roughly 8%, with heating oil concentrated heavily in the Northeast.

## The math for one person

The mechanism behind the divergence is worth understanding, not just the direction. Electricity-heated homes were projected to pay more largely because electricity prices themselves had been climbing broadly — driven partly by rising grid demand, including from data centers, a trend I've covered separately on why electric bills have been climbing. Natural gas prices stayed comparatively stable, keeping gas-heated homes' bills roughly flat year over year. Propane and heating oil, both more exposed to crude-oil-linked pricing, were projected to ease from the prior winter's levels — good news specifically for that smaller, regionally concentrated group of households.

Propane's within-season pattern is its own useful data point: the EIA projected prices starting around $2.13 per gallon in October and climbing to roughly $2.58 by March, a seasonal pattern driven by demand ramping through the coldest months rather than a single flat price holding all winter. If you heat with propane, locking in a fall delivery price can matter more than the year-over-year comparison alone suggests.

<InfoBox type="tip">
If your home is heated by electricity, the story to watch isn't really "winter fuel prices" — it's the broader electricity-price trend, which has more to do with grid demand and generation costs than with anything specific to the heating season. Check your utility's rate filings, not just seasonal outlooks.
</InfoBox>

## Here's What I'd Actually Do

1. **Identify your specific heating fuel and check that fuel's specific EIA projection**, not a blended national average that doesn't describe your situation.
2. **If you heat with propane or oil, ask about locking in a fall delivery price** before the seasonal climb toward peak winter pricing.
3. **Run a basic home energy audit if you haven't in a few years** — insulation and air-sealing gaps often have a faster payback than people expect.
4. **Budget for a colder-than-average winter, not the base-case projection**, since weather is the biggest single swing factor and it's inherently unpredictable months out.
5. **Set aside a specific "winter buffer" in your budget in the fall**, rather than absorbing the bill as a surprise each month it arrives.
6. **Check back for the 2026-27 EIA Winter Fuels Outlook in mid-October** and update your planning once the confirmed figures replace this post's last-winter baseline.
