## What actually happened

Nothing new triggered this post — it's the collision of two things I've covered separately finally getting the joint treatment it deserves. Roth conversions get a lot of attention every fall as a year-end tax-planning move: move money from a traditional IRA or 401(k) into a Roth account, pay ordinary income tax on the converted amount now, and let it grow tax-free from that point forward. For people expecting higher tax rates later, or wanting to reduce future required minimum distributions, that trade can make real sense.

What gets less attention is that the income from a conversion doesn't just affect your tax bracket — it also counts toward your Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI) for Medicare's Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount, IRMAA. If you're on Medicare or will be within two years, a conversion sized without checking against IRMAA thresholds can trigger a real, multi-year premium increase that easily outweighs the tax benefit the conversion was supposed to deliver.

## The math for one person

Two mechanics matter here, and they compound in a way that catches people off guard. First: IRMAA isn't assessed on your current income — it's assessed on a two-year-old tax return. The premium you pay in 2028 is based on the MAGI reported on your 2026 tax return. That lag means a conversion decision made this year has consequences that don't show up in your monthly budget until two full years later, which makes it easy to convert without connecting the dots at the time.

Second, and more consequential: IRMAA is structured as a cliff, not a smooth slope. Medicare defines a series of MAGI thresholds, and each one has an associated flat surcharge added to your Part B and Part D premiums. Land $1 over a threshold, and you pay the full surcharge for that entire tier — not a prorated amount reflecting how far over you are. A conversion that lands you at $1 over a tier boundary costs the same in surcharge as one that lands you $20,000 over it, which is precisely why sizing the conversion precisely, rather than roughly, matters so much.

<InfoBox type="warning">
Because a Roth conversion is irreversible under current law, an IRMAA-triggering conversion mistake can't be corrected after the fact the way it could before 2018, when recharacterization let you undo a conversion you regretted. Model the MAGI impact before you convert, not after.
</InfoBox>

## Why converting can still be right

None of this is an argument against Roth conversions — it's an argument for sizing them deliberately. The standard, well-established version of this strategy is often called "filling up the bracket": convert exactly enough to use up the remaining room in your current tax bracket (or to stay just under an IRMAA threshold), and stop there for the year, potentially spreading a larger total conversion across several years instead of doing it all at once.

The years before you or your spouse turn 65 and enroll in Medicare are often the highest-value window for larger conversions, precisely because IRMAA doesn't apply yet — the two-year lookback means conversions done well before Medicare eligibility never trigger a surcharge on your own account at all.

## Here's What I'd Actually Do

1. **Model your MAGI for the conversion year against current IRMAA thresholds before you convert anything** — not after you file, when it's too late to adjust.
2. **If you're within two years of Medicare eligibility, treat IRMAA as a hard constraint on conversion size**, not an afterthought to check once the tax math looks good.
3. **Consider spreading a large conversion across multiple years** instead of one lump sum, to avoid stacking a big one-time MAGI spike against a single year's thresholds.
4. **Verify current IRMAA thresholds directly at medicare.gov or with a tax professional** before finalizing any conversion amount — thresholds adjust periodically and the exact current figures matter.
5. **Remember the two-year lag when budgeting** — a 2026 conversion's premium impact shows up in 2028, so don't assume "no change yet" means "no change coming."
6. **Get a second opinion from a fee-only financial planner or CPA for any conversion above a few tens of thousands of dollars** — the permanence of the decision justifies the cost of a professional review.
