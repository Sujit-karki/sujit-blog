Writing about running AI at home usually compares graphics cards, or quotes a
GPU's rated wattage off a spec sheet. Neither answers the question a person
actually has, which is: if I use this thing, what happens to my electricity bill?

So I measured it. A background sampler polled the GPU's power draw four times a
second while four models generated text, on the same laptop used for [the
numeracy benchmark](/posts/local-ai-money-math-2026) and [the tax
test](/posts/ai-tax-brackets-more-context-worse-2026) on this site — an Acer
Nitro 5 with a GTX 1650 Ti.

## The number

At the Nepal Electricity Authority domestic rate of 12 rupees per kilowatt-hour:

    ["phi3.5:3.8b", "22.2", "35.7 W", "0.42", "5.01"]

A million tokens is a great deal of text — roughly seven or eight full-length
novels. Producing it costs **between three and five rupees**.

Put another way: **one rupee buys about 1.8 hours of continuous generation.** The
electricity is not the constraint. It is not close to being the constraint.

### The same numbers in US cents

Rupees are the wrong currency for most readers of this site, so here it is in
dollars. **Energy per token does not depend on where you live** — only the price
of that energy does, and the US price is published: the Bureau of Labor
Statistics tracks the average price of electricity per kilowatt-hour as series
`APU000072610`. As of July 2026 it stands at **19.7 cents**.

  caption="Same measurement, US city average electricity price (19.7 c/kWh, BLS, July 2026)"

**Five cents per million tokens.** American electricity is about two and a half
times the Nepali domestic rate — 19.7 cents against roughly 7.9 cents equivalent
— so the same computation costs more in Ohio than in Kathmandu. It is still five
cents. Nothing about that changes the conclusion.

It is worth noting which direction that cuts, though. The economics of running a
model at home are *better* in a country with cheap electricity, and Nepal has
some of the cheapest domestic power in the region. If local inference ever
becomes genuinely competitive on capability, the places that benefit most are the
ones where a kilowatt-hour is cheap and a cloud subscription is expensive in
local wages.

## The model that sips power costs the most

Look at the last row of that table again, because it inverts the intuition the
whole "wattage" framing encourages.

`phi3.5:3.8b` draws **35.7 watts** under load — about 22% *less* than the other
three, which sit near 45. On a power meter it is the gentle one. Yet it is the
**most expensive model per token**, by a margin of 61%.

The reason is arithmetic. Energy per token is **power divided by throughput**,
and phi3.5 generates at 22.2 tokens per second against roughly 52 for the
others. Drawing 22% less power while taking 2.3 times as long is a losing trade.
It holds the GPU for longer, and duration beats wattage.

<InfoBox type="tip">
This is why a wattage figure on its own tells you nothing about running cost. A
model that idles the card at low power for a long time can cost more than one
that runs it hard and finishes. The number to compare is energy per unit of work,
not watts.
</InfoBox>

There is a sharper edge to this. In [the tax
benchmark](/posts/ai-tax-brackets-more-context-worse-2026), `phi3.5:3.8b` was the
**only model of the four to score above zero** — 51% correct where the others got
nothing right. So the one model worth using is also the one that costs the most
to run. On this hardware, accuracy is not free; it is about 60% more expensive
per token. It is still five rupees per million.

## Against the cloud, honestly

Taking the US electricity price above, the cheapest model costs **$0.051 per
million tokens** to run:

  caption="Local electricity cost against published API pricing, at US electricity rates"
  headers={["Option", "Per 1M output tokens", "Ratio"]}
  rows={[
    ["qwen2.5:3b on this laptop", "$0.051", "baseline"],
    ["Claude Haiku 4.5", "$5.00", "98x"],
    ["Claude Sonnet 5", "$10.00", "196x"],
    ["Claude Opus 5", "$25.00", "489x"]
  ]}
  highlightCol={1}
/>

At the Nepali rate the same ratios are roughly two and a half times larger again —
243x, 486x and 1,216x — because the electricity is cheaper while the API price is
the same everywhere. That is the quiet part of global API pricing: a dollar is a
dollar in Kathmandu and in California, but a kilowatt-hour is not.

Those ratios are real, and they are also the most misleading numbers in this
post if left without qualification. Three things they do not account for:

**It is electricity against a retail price.** The API figure covers datacentre
hardware, model training, serving capacity, bandwidth, support and profit. The
local figure covers none of those, because you already paid for the laptop. A
fair comparison would amortise the machine — and at these volumes the hardware
dominates completely.

**The models are not comparable.** A 3B model quantised to fit 4 GB is not
Claude Opus 5, and the two previous experiments on this site measured exactly
how badly these models fail at ordinary money arithmetic. Three of the four
scored zero on a tax calculation. Cheap output you cannot trust is not a bargain.

**It is GPU-only.** CPU, RAM, display and power-supply losses are real and I have
no wall-plug meter to capture them, so the true figure is somewhat higher. It is
a floor, not a total.

## What this does and does not tell you

The defensible claim is narrow: **once you own the hardware, the marginal cost of
running a model on it rounds to nothing.** Three rupees per million tokens is not
a budget line. It is not worth thinking about.

That matters because it removes running cost from the decision entirely. If you
are choosing between a local model and an API, the real questions are capability,
privacy, and whether you already own a machine that can run it. Electricity is
not one of the questions, and any argument that leans on it — in either direction
— is arguing about a number too small to matter.

The corollary is less comfortable. If electricity is free and the hardware is
already bought, the only remaining cost of a wrong answer is the wrong answer
itself. On this hardware, [that turns out to be the expensive
part](/posts/ai-tax-brackets-more-context-worse-2026).

## Methodology

The measurement script and the raw results are in this site's repository under
`research/`, and the run reproduces on any machine with an NVIDIA GPU and Ollama.

**Hardware.** Acer Nitro 5, NVIDIA GTX 1650 Ti (4 GB VRAM), 8 GB system RAM,
Ollama 0.33.2. All four models ran fully on the GPU.

**Sampling.** `nvidia-smi` reports instantaneous power draw. A background thread
polls it every 250 ms for the duration of each generation, so each figure is a
mean over that specific request rather than a nameplate rating. Five generations
per model, each producing 608 to 768 tokens.

**Marginal, not total.** The laptop draws power whether or not it is inferring.
The idle baseline — **3.51 W**, measured before the run — is subtracted, so the
headline figures are the *additional* energy inference costs. Total draw is
recorded alongside in the dataset and is roughly 8% higher.

**Rates.** Electricity at 12 NPR/kWh. Currency converted at the Nepal Rastra Bank
published rate for 7 September 2026 (USD sell 151.48). API prices are Anthropic's
published per-million-token output rates.

**Limits.** GPU only, as described above. One machine, one GPU, four small
models — throughput and therefore cost per token will differ on other hardware.
The script accepts any tariff via `--rate`, so the calculation reproduces at your
own electricity price rather than mine.

**Disclosure.** The models are the subject being measured, not the author. The
commentary was drafted with AI assistance; the experiment design, the measurement
method and the conclusions are mine.
