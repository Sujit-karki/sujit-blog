I've covered Bitcoin's ETF flows on this site. Solana's spot ETFs are the newer story, and the interesting part isn't that they exist — it's that they launched with staking built in from day one, which neither of the bigger two managed at launch. That's a real structural difference, and it's worth doing the actual fee math instead of taking the marketing number at face value.

## What actually happened

US spot Solana ETFs launched October 28, 2025 (Bitwise's BSOL began trading October 23), following the SEC's adoption of generic listing standards for crypto ETFs on September 18, 2025 — a process shift that let issuers list new crypto ETFs faster than the individual-approval path Bitcoin and Ethereum funds went through. The genuinely new part: these funds launched already staking the SOL they hold, passing a share of the protocol-level staking reward through to shareholders as part of the fund's return, something neither the first Bitcoin ETFs (Bitcoin isn't a proof-of-stake asset, so this doesn't apply) nor the first-generation Ethereum ETFs offered at launch.

As of August 2026, three issuers had funds live: Bitwise (BSOL), VanEck (VSOL), and Grayscale (GSOL, converted from an existing product with its fee cut to 0.19% sponsor plus a 7% staking fee effective June 25, 2026). Bitwise's BSOL was the largest, with reported assets around $616 million, a 0.20% sponsor fee, and a net staking reward rate of 5.84% (gross 6.21%) with essentially all fund assets staked. VanEck's VSOL was considerably smaller — around $14.7 million — with a higher 0.30% expense ratio, a lower gross staking yield of 4.46% (net 4.25%), and only about 72% of assets actually staked.

## The math for one person

The number that gets marketed is the gross staking reward rate — the yield SOL earns natively from validating the network, before any fund-level costs. The number that actually lands in your account is smaller, for two mechanical reasons that stack on top of each other.

First, not every dollar in the fund is staked. BSOL runs close to 100% staked, but VSOL runs closer to 72% — meaning roughly 28% of VSOL's assets earn no staking reward at all, dragging down the blended yield the whole fund actually delivers. Second, whatever staking reward the fund does earn gets reduced by the fund's own expense ratio (and, for some funds, an additional staking-specific fee layered on top of the base management fee). Do both subtractions and BSOL's 6.21% gross becomes 5.84% net; VSOL's 4.46% gross becomes 4.25% net on the staked portion, but the unstaked 28% pulls the fund's overall blended yield lower still.

<InfoBox type="tip">
When comparing staked crypto ETFs, always ask for three numbers, not one: the gross protocol staking rate, the percentage of fund assets actually staked, and the total fee load (sponsor fee plus any separate staking fee). A fund quoting only the first number is quoting the least useful one.
</InfoBox>

None of this touches the part of the return that actually dominates: SOL's own price. At roughly $76 as of mid-August 2026 (check a live price before relying on this — it moves daily), SOL's month-to-month price swings have routinely exceeded a full year of staking yield in either direction. A 5%–6% annual yield is a real, incremental benefit over holding a non-yielding crypto ETF. It is not, by itself, an investment thesis.

## The case against chasing yield

The obvious pull here is comparing 5.84% to a savings account or Treasury yield and concluding the "risk-adjusted" case is compelling. It isn't a fair comparison. A staked Solana ETF still carries full exposure to SOL's price, which is dramatically more volatile than either a savings account or a Treasury bond, and the staking yield doesn't hedge that volatility at all — it's additive return on top of a still-fully-exposed principal, not downside protection.

There's also a genuine tradeoff against self-custody staking: doing it yourself typically captures the full gross yield with no fund-level fee drag, but requires understanding lockup and unstaking-queue mechanics, running or delegating to a validator, and managing your own tax reporting — friction a lot of investors reasonably decide isn't worth it for a few percentage points of extra yield.

## Ethereum: the same fee math, a thinner yield

Ethereum's staking story runs on the same mechanics with different numbers. Roughly **a third of all ETH supply** is now staked — tens of millions of ETH across hundreds of thousands of active validators, removed from liquid supply and earning a yield instead of sitting idle.

The catch is that more validators split the same reward pool. The base staking APR has drifted down to around **2.6%**, well below the 5%+ levels seen in 2023 — so ETH starts from a thinner gross yield than SOL before any fund takes its cut.

And the cut is bigger in shape. Staked-ETH ETFs don't pass 100% of staking rewards through: representative fund filings describe an aggregate staking fee equal to a meaningful share of gross staking rewards — often in the high teens as a percentage — layered on top of the ordinary management fee. A ~2.6% gross yield minus an ~18%-of-rewards staking fee minus a management fee leaves a net figure noticeably below the headline. Attractive versus zero, but not the number in the marketing.

Meanwhile ETH has traded well below its 2025 all-time high of roughly $4,946 for most of 2026, and its price can move double digits on crypto-wide sentiment alone. A 2–3% annual yield doesn't offset swings of that size in either direction — the same lesson as SOL, with less yield to soften it.

## Here's What I'd Actually Do

1. **Decide on SOL exposure first, staking yield second.** The yield is a real bonus on top of an investment decision, not a reason to make the decision.
2. **Compare net yield, not gross, across funds** — the staked percentage and total fee load can turn a higher headline number into a lower actual one.
3. **Re-check current AUM, fees, and staked share before investing** — all three move regularly, and a number from a few months ago can already be stale.
4. **If you're set on staking yourself instead of through an ETF, understand the unstaking-queue delay** before you need the liquidity.
5. **Track your cost basis carefully regardless of which route you take** — see my [crypto 1099-DA cost basis guide](/posts/crypto-1099-da-cost-basis-2026) for why this matters more than most investors expect.
6. **Keep crypto, staked or not, in the speculative slice of your portfolio** — money you could genuinely afford to lose, not your emergency fund or near-term savings.
