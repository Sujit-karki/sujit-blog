Open enrollment season means the same email lands in every inbox: pick your health plan, pick your pretax account, don't think about it too hard. I'd push back on that last part. The HSA-versus-FSA decision compounds for years if you get it wrong, and 2027's numbers just gave HSA savers a real bump.

## What actually happened

The IRS confirmed 2027 HSA contribution limits in Revenue Procedure 2026-24, released May 29, 2026 — notably early and notably official, unlike the tax-bracket projections everyone's still guessing at for the same year. Self-only HSA coverage can now shelter up to $4,500 pretax, up from $4,400 in 2026. Family coverage jumps to $9,000, up from $8,750. The 55-and-older catch-up contribution stays fixed at $1,000, a figure that — unlike the base limits — isn't indexed for inflation and hasn't moved in years.

The 2027 minimum deductible and out-of-pocket maximum for a plan to qualify as an HDHP also moved: a minimum deductible of $1,750 self-only / $3,500 family, and an out-of-pocket cap of $8,700 self-only / $17,400 family. Those numbers matter because they define whether your specific health plan even makes you HSA-eligible in the first place — a plan with a deductible below that floor doesn't qualify, no matter how much you'd like to contribute.

## The math for one person

The single biggest structural difference between the two accounts isn't the contribution limit — it's what happens to unused money. An HSA is yours permanently. Contribute $3,000 this year, spend $800 on actual medical costs, and the remaining $2,200 rolls over indefinitely, keeps earning interest or investment returns if your HSA provider allows investing (many do once you hit a minimum balance), and stays with you even if you switch jobs or health plans entirely. It's the only account in the tax code with triple tax advantage: pretax contributions, tax-free growth, and tax-free withdrawals for qualified medical expenses — better treatment than either a traditional or Roth retirement account gets on its own.

An FSA is the opposite by design. It's a "use it or lose it" account: contribute for the plan year, and unless your employer's specific plan allows a carryover (capped at $680 for 2026) or a short grace period, unspent money reverts to your employer at year-end. That's not a minor footnote — it's the entire reason FSA elections require actually forecasting your medical spend for the coming year, while HSA elections don't require any forecasting at all, since there's no penalty for contributing more than you end up spending.

<InfoBox type="tip">
The forecasting burden is the real practical difference day to day. With an HSA, when in doubt, contribute more — worst case, it sits invested for a decade until you need it. With an FSA, when in doubt, contribute less than your best guess, because overestimating is the costly mistake, not underestimating.
</InfoBox>

The catch is eligibility. You can't simply choose an HSA the way you choose an FSA — it requires enrolling in a qualifying high-deductible health plan. If your employer only offers a standard PPO, an HSA isn't on the table regardless of how much you'd like the tax treatment, and the FSA becomes the only pretax option for predictable medical costs.

## When an FSA still wins

I don't want to oversell the HSA, because for a specific kind of household the FSA is genuinely the better fit, and it's worth naming why plainly rather than treating this as a foregone conclusion.

Dependent-care FSAs are a separate account entirely, worth mentioning because they're easy to conflate with the health FSA: the limit is $7,500, set by statute rather than indexed for inflation, and it covers childcare and eldercare costs, not medical expenses. If you have kids in daycare, that's likely worth electing regardless of which health-side account you choose.

## Here's What I'd Actually Do

1. **Check what your employer actually offers before comparing accounts in the abstract** — if there's no HDHP option, the HSA decision is already made for you.
2. **If an HDHP is available, run the premium difference first.** A meaningfully lower HDHP premium can offset a higher deductible even before counting the HSA's tax benefits.
3. **For an FSA, estimate conservatively.** Look at last year's actual medical spend, not this year's hoped-for healthy streak, and elect slightly under that number.
4. **For an HSA, elect as much as you comfortably can up to the cap** — there's no downside to over-contributing since nothing expires, only the opportunity cost of the cash itself.
5. **If you're 55 or older, don't forget the $1,000 catch-up** — it's easy to miss because, unlike the base limit, it hasn't changed in years and isn't automatically prompted by most enrollment software.
6. **Revisit this every open enrollment**, since HDHP thresholds, HSA limits, and your own health situation can all shift the right answer year to year.
