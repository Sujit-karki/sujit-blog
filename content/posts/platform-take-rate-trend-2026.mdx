[A previous piece](/posts/platform-take-rates-2026) measured what eight platforms
keep per dollar transacted, for one year. One year cannot tell you whether a
number is a trend or a coincidence. This is the same calculation run across
three.

The headline finding did not survive contact with the second and third years —
not because it was wrong, but because it was incomplete in a way that mattered.

## One year was not enough

The FY2025 snapshot showed Fiverr keeping the most, at 40.2%, and it was
tempting to read that as platforms tightening their grip. Adding FY2023 and
FY2024 says something more specific and less dramatic.

Take rates did not rise across the board. They rose at two companies, barely
moved at four, and fell at two. What separates them is not size, sector or
seniority. It is whether the business was growing.

## The four that can be compared

Only the net-basis marketplaces measure the same thing as each other. Uber,
Lyft and DoorDash book substantial revenue gross, so their ratios are a
different quantity wearing the same name, and mixing them into a comparison
would be the error this whole exercise exists to avoid.

That leaves four. Ordered by how much their take rate moved:

The ordering is perfect and inverse. The most contracted business raised its
take the most; the fastest-growing one is the only one that fell. There is no
crossover, no exception, and the gap between the two groups is not marginal —
roughly three points against roughly a tenth of a point.

Upwork is the entry that a single year would have missed entirely. Between
FY2024 and FY2025 its take rate moved 0.37 points, which reads as noise. Across
the full period it moved 2.92, nearly all of it in FY2024. Measuring one year
and calling it flat would have been wrong in a way no amount of care within that
year could have caught.

<InfoBox type="info">
Four companies is not a law. It is a pattern with no counterexample in the set
measured, which is a reason to look further rather than a reason to conclude.
The section below on what would change my mind is not a formality.
</InfoBox>

## It is not the commission

The obvious reading — shrinking platforms squeeze sellers harder — is wrong, and
the companies' own filings say so. Both of the risers break revenue into a
marketplace line and a services line, and the two moved in opposite directions.

**Fiverr, FY2024 to FY2025.** Its marketplace take rate, the cut it takes on a
transaction, went from 27.61% to 27.72%. That is 0.11 points, essentially flat.
Services revenue over the same denominator went from 8.06% to 12.43%. Total take
rose 4.49 points, and **97.5% of it was services**. Marketplace revenue actually
fell 1.8% while services revenue grew 50.9%.

Anyone writing "Fiverr raised its take rate" from the headline figure would be
describing something that did not happen.

**Etsy, FY2024 to FY2025.** The split is less extreme and more interesting.
Marketplace take rose 0.79 points and services rose 1.10, so 58% of the increase
came from services — advertising, shipping labels, payments — and 42% from the
marketplace itself.

But even Etsy's marketplace share is not a fee increase. Its marketplace revenue
*fell* 0.7% that year. The ratio rose because gross merchandise sales fell 5.3%,
faster than the revenue did. A denominator shrinking faster than its numerator
raises a ratio without anyone changing a price.

That is the mechanism behind most of this. A marketplace losing volume keeps
more per remaining dollar partly because its costs of being a marketplace do not
fall in step, and mostly because it sells sellers more things that are not
commission.

## The year Fiverr does not have

Fiverr has no FY2023 bar, and the reason is worth more than the missing data
point.

Its FY2023 annual report gives company-wide "GMV" of $1,134.7 million. From
FY2024 the metric is named "marketplace GMV" and reported as $1,097.6 million,
described as down 2.0% on the prior year — which implies roughly $1,120 million
for 2023 on the new basis, not $1,134.7 million. The definition narrowed, and no
filing states the restated 2023 figure outright.

Putting those two numbers in one series would have produced a 3% decline that
never happened, in the very company whose FY2025 figure is the most eye-catching
in the dataset. The extraction script now refuses to do it: the platform is
excluded before FY2024 and the run prints the exclusion, because a gap nobody
mentions reads as a company that did not exist.

The general point is that a metric's name is not a promise of continuity.
Multi-year comparisons of non-GAAP operating metrics are exposed to redefinition
in a way that GAAP line items are not, and the only defence is reading the
definition in each filing rather than trusting the label.

## What would change my mind

Four comparable companies, three years. The honest description is a consistent
pattern in a small set, and here is what would break it:

- **A shrinking marketplace with a falling take rate.** None exists in this set.
  One would show the relationship is not general.
- **Services growth at the growing platforms too.** If eBay and Airbnb are also
  expanding services revenue but their take rates are flat because volume
  outruns it, then the story is "everyone sells more services" rather than
  "shrinking platforms do".
- **A fourth year.** If Etsy's volume stabilises and its take rate keeps
  climbing, the mechanism is pricing, not arithmetic.

The dataset is published so that the first two can be checked by someone who
disagrees with me.

## Methodology

Revenue comes from XBRL company facts on `data.sec.gov` — the figure each
company tagged itself. Gross volume is not in XBRL: it is a non-GAAP operating
metric stated in the narrative under a different name at every company, so it is
extracted from the filing text with the source sentence and filing URL recorded
on every row.

**Selecting the year is the part that is easy to get wrong.** These key-metrics
tables put two or three fiscal years side by side, and the column order is not
consistent — Uber's runs 2024 then 2025, Etsy's runs 2025 then 2024, and eBay's
FY2023 filing is a two-panel layout comparing 2023-vs-2022 and 2022-vs-2021 in
the same row. An earlier version of the script hardcoded a column position per
company, which is correct for exactly one filing and silently wrong for every
other year. Columns are now matched against the fiscal years in the table header
and selected by year, and if the cell count and the header years do not
reconcile, the run stops instead of guessing.

**The cross-check.** eBay publishes its own take rate, defined the same way, for
every year here: 13.81%, 13.77% and 13.94%. Recomputing from the underlying
figures reproduces all three exactly. Etsy publishes 24.2% and 22.3% for FY2025
and FY2024 and both reproduce, but it did not publish a numeric take rate before
its FY2025 filing, so FY2023 rests on eBay alone. Fiverr and Upwork publish a
*marketplace* take rate — a narrower numerator over the same denominator — so
their 27.7% and 18.7% are legitimately lower than the figures here, and the
script does not assert equality against them.

**Reproducibility.** The script is
[`research/platform_take.py`](https://github.com/Sujit-karki/sujit-blog/blob/main/research/platform_take.py),
run as `python platform_take.py --fiscal-year 2024`. It caches each filing on
first run, keyed by year. The datasets are `platform-take.csv`,
`platform-take-2024.csv` and `platform-take-2023.csv`.

**Disclosure.** Figures are computed from primary filings by the script above;
the analysis and conclusions are mine. The commentary was drafted with AI
assistance. No language model produced any figure in this post.
